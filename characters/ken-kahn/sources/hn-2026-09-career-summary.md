## The career spine, verbatim

From [49676062](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49676062):

> Ken arrived at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in the 1970s, where Marvin
> Minsky and Seymour Papert served on his PhD committee. His thesis "Creation of Computer
> Animation from Story Descriptions" was a very early example of generative AI.
>
> During the 1970s (specifically between 1973 and 1979), Ken taught artificial intelligence
> to elementary school children as a graduate researcher in the MIT AI Laboratory's Logo
> Group.
>
> He developed a distributed concurrent constraint visual programming language called
> Pictorial Janus in 1990-1991.
>
> From 1995-present, he developed a visual programming language called ToonTalk, that looks
> and feels like a 3D video game, for teaching programming and high level CS concepts like
> concurrency and message passing to kids.
>
> 53 years after he started teaching Logo to kids with Seymour Papert at the MIT AI Lab Logo
> Group, he's still teaching AI Programming and Computer Science to kids, now with Snap! and
> his eCraft2Learn project.

## One correction the archive makes to the comment

The HN line says Minsky and Papert "served on his PhD committee." The fact-check already in
[`../CARD.yml`](../CARD.yml) is more precise and stays: the **advisor was Carl Hewitt** (MIT
DSpace 1721.1/16012; Mathematics Genealogy), with Minsky and Papert reporting on the
committee. Both statements are true; the archive's is the one to quote.

Hewitt was also on the "Marketing vs. Innovation" panel at NPUC 1993 the same afternoon Ken
spoke — see [`npuc-almaden-panel.md`](npuc-almaden-panel.md).

## Open question: which NPUC, and was ToonTalk on screen

Don's recollection is meeting Ken at NPUC **showing ToonTalk**. The archived First Annual
program (15 June 1993) lists his talk as *Sketches and Animations as Programs*, given under
the company name **Animated Programs, Inc.** — while the HN comment dates ToonTalk itself
1995–present.

Three readings, none averaged:

1. The 1993 talk showed ToonTalk's direct precursor under a different name, and Don's memory
   attached the later name to the earlier demo. The company name supports this.
2. ToonTalk existed earlier than 1995 in prototype and 1995 is a publication date.
3. Don and Ken were both at a later NPUC as well, and the ToonTalk demo was there.

Resolvable by asking Ken, or from Ted Selker's claimed archive of "transcriptions, videos and
audios of 7 years" of NPUC.
