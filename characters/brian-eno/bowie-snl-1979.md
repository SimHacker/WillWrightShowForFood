# Bowie on SNL, Dec 15, 1979 — the video-puppet night (a thread to explore with Brian) 🎛️📺

On **Saturday Night Live, December 15, 1979** (host Martin Sheen), David Bowie performed **three
songs** backed by the downtown avant-garde duo **Klaus Nomi** (operatic countertenor) and **Joey
Arias**. It is one of the strangest, most beautiful art-performances ever smuggled onto live network
TV — and one of it, "Boys Keep Swinging," is a **Bowie/Eno co-write**, so it's a real, warm door into
a conversation with Brian about that whole period.

It's also the origin of the show's **["video puppet"](../../repo-shows/will-wright-premiere/performance-and-culture.yml)**
performance kind (`#puppet_kinds` → `puppet.canonical_ancestor`): a human performing live through a
cheap, seams-showing composited body.

## The three songs

### 1. "The Man Who Sold the World" — carried on, immobilized
Bowie opened in a **rigid, sculptural Dada-esque tuxedo** that pinned his arms to his sides; he had to
be **carried to the microphone** and set down like a mannequin. A song about meeting a self you thought
had died, performed by a man made into a **living puppet who cannot move** — the machine holds the body,
the voice does everything. (Written 1970; the SNL rendition is the emotional heart of the set.)

**The retrospective ache (handle with care — this is interpretation, not fact):** Klaus Nomi sang
beside him that night. **Nomi died of AIDS-related illness in August 1983** — one of the first public
figures lost to the epidemic, at a moment when fear and stigma led much of the scene to keep its
distance, and he died largely isolated. Heard today, "*...I never lost control / You're face to face /
With the man who sold the world*" and the whole set's mortality carry a weight nobody could have known
in 1979. Don's read: this is the most poignant performance on the tape. **We do not assert who did or
didn't stand by Nomi** — that's contested and personal; we'd raise it as elegy, with respect, not
accusation. (Joey Arias has spoken publicly about the era; *The Nomi Song*, 2004, is the documentary.)

*Companion viewing (Don's rec): **"The Man Who Sold the World"** hits especially hard after watching
**_The Nomi Song_**, and pairs beautifully with the **Leigh Bowery** documentary — the same lineage of
persona, mask, and gender-performance the show keeps returning to.*

**The after-party that led nowhere (public record via *The Nomi Song*, told as elegy):** By the accounts
in the film, the people around Nomi believed he'd *finally made it* — a slot beside Bowie on live network
TV is the kind of break careers are built on. *The Nomi Song* gathers **first-hand interviews** recalling
the elation, the great after-party, the warm talk of what might come next — and then the quiet: the
collaboration didn't continue, and the follow-up that the night seemed to promise never came. The people
who were there remember it with **real sadness** — a break that flickered and didn't catch. We raise it
exactly that way: it's already on the public record as a sad turn, so we don't need to embellish or
accuse. **No villain, no unfounded claims** — just the ordinary heartbreak of the industry and a missed
collaboration between extraordinary artists who made *one* spectacular thing together and never got to
make more. Let the people who were in the room say it; we hold it gently.

### 2. "TVC 15" — the TV that eats your girlfriend *(a story to ask Brian about, not his song)*
**Not an Eno track** — "TVC 15" is from *Station to Station* (1976), produced by Bowie & Harry Maslin,
pre-Berlin-Trilogy. But the *story* is pure catnip: the lyric is about a man whose **girlfriend is
devoured by a television set**, and he contemplates climbing *into* the TV to be with her. Documented
inspirations: **a dream/hallucination of Iggy Pop's** (a partner consumed by a TV) and the wall of
televisions in **Bowie's film *The Man Who Fell to Earth*** (1976). *(Adjacent lore, flagged: Bowie was
one of the few who stood by Iggy through his mid-'70s hospitalization, before they decamped to Berlin —
worth asking Brian about, gently, as the human backdrop to the era.)*

Great cross-link to hand Brian: guitarist **Carlos Alomar** said Bowie wanted TVC 15 "*fucked up like
when we did [the Lodger track] 'Boys Keep Swinging', kind of loose and stupid*" — so the **Eno song was
already the touchstone** for "loose and stupid on purpose." On SNL, Bowie sang it in a **pencil skirt
and heels** while a **stuffed pink poodle held a TV screen in its mouth**.

### 3. "Boys Keep Swinging" — the video puppet whips it out *(Bowie/Eno co-write)*
The finale, and **the one Brian co-wrote** (from *Lodger*, 1979 — the third of the Berlin Trilogy).
Bowie's real head was **chroma-keyed onto a crude, flailing hand-drawn marionette body** — a low-tech
**video puppet**. At the end the puppet "**whipped it out**" (an erection drawn on the puppet's crotch)
and Bowie flashed a **cherubic grin** at having slipped it past the NBC censors on live national TV.
(Don posted the clip — his most-viewed video — until NBC blocked it: [youtu.be/5yrXg3zTseY](https://youtu.be/5yrXg3zTseY).)

## The question for Brian: what was "Boys Keep Swinging" *saying*?

It's art, so there's no single answer — but Brian was **in the room writing it**, so the real questions are:

- **Then:** how did the two of you *think* about it at the time — what was the intent behind the
  swagger?
- **Now:** how does he see it in retrospect, decades on?
- **Reinterpreted:** how would he frame it for today's audience?

**Don's reading (offered as Don's, to test against Brian's):** it isn't sexist — it's a **satire of
male carte-blanche**. "Boys keep swinging / boys always work it out" is the ironic voice of a world
where boys can do *anything* — literally **whip their johnson out on live TV and grin about getting
away with it** — because the rules don't apply to them. The **official 1979 video** (dir. David Mallet)
makes the critique explicit: Bowie plays *all three* backing singers **in drag**, then tears off the
wigs and smears the lipstick — masculinity as a costume you can put on and rip off. The SNL puppet
finale is the same joke in the flesh: **get the forbidden thing past the gatekeepers, and smile.** That
"got-away-with-it" delight is the exact seam between this bit and the whole RepoShow ethos — and Brian,
a noted creature of the night, tends to relish precisely that.
