# Reading the prototype's rhetoric, nine years before it had a name

*Don Hopkins, first person. The account of why the 1998 code got flagged at all — the piece the
theory spine can't supply, because the theory didn't exist yet.*

## What happened

In the prototype SimAntics code, one woman kissing another got slapped. No design document said
to do that. No person on the team intended it. I read it as homophobic anyway, and I could read
it because I'd lived it as a gay man.

I didn't have the vocabulary. Ian Bogost hadn't coined **procedural rhetoric** yet — that arrives
in *Persuasive Games* in 2007, nine years later. The queer theory that Alexander Avila's essay
later handed me wasn't in my hands either. What I had instead was the intuition, from working
with Will and on SimCity, and before that with Ben Shneiderman on hypermedia, and with NeWS and
HyperLook: **rules that execute persuade.** I knew that in my hands long before I could say it in
somebody else's terms. I thought there was an interesting analysis and discussion to be had, and
I couldn't name what I was looking at.

## The theory I did have

Mine wasn't about rhetoric. It was about suicide.

A non-judgmental place is worth something. The Sims could be one — somewhere a kid could play a
life that nobody in their actual life would let them have, and not be told it was strange. So
supporting same-sex relationships **without raising an eyebrow, without the game acting as though
it were anything but normal**, was not an accident or a side effect. That was the point, and it
was intentional. Not tolerance as a feature. Normality as a default. Against loneliness, against
despair, against kids killing themselves.

That is why the mechanism mattered more than the representation. A game that congratulates you
for a same-sex relationship has still marked it as remarkable. A game that just lets it happen,
with the same rules and the same animations and the same neighbors gossiping about it, has not.

## Why the two gifts landed so hard

Bogost gave me the term for what I had been doing with my hands for twenty years. Avila gave me
the frame for why it mattered to the people it mattered to. Neither of them was telling me
something I hadn't done; they were telling me what it was called and why it worked. That is a
particular kind of delight, and it is also what makes the paper writable at all — I can finally
describe the 1998 reading in language other people share.

## And then the comments

Avila's essay drew 1,712 comments, and reading them was not a pleasant surprise so much as
**measurable confirmation that the experiment succeeded.** People describing, unprompted and in
their own words, having discovered something about themselves in a dollhouse in 2000 because
nothing in the software treated them as unusual. That's the result. The design intent from 1998
and the reported experience twenty years later are the two ends of the same claim, and the
comment corpus is the evidence in the middle.

## Why this section exists in the paper

The paper's spine is other people's theory — Bogost, Murray, Frasca, McCloud, Papert, Sihvonen.
This is the one part only I can write. It establishes that the rhetoric in the prototype was
**legible before it was theorized**, which is the strongest available argument that procedural
rhetoric is describing something real rather than supplying a lens after the fact. The code made
an argument. A player who had been on the receiving end of that argument in life recognized it in
the rules. That happened nine years before the term was published.
