# Objects that argue — the Wedding Playset chapter

*The paper's case chapter. Downloadable content for The Sims 1 that makes arguments in rules,
none of them stated anywhere in the game — and the authoring tools that handed the same power to
everybody else. All of it built before Don had the term for what he was doing. See*
[`reading-the-prototype.md`](reading-the-prototype.md) *for why the vocabulary arrived late.*

**A note on scale, because these are not comparable units.** **SimProv** is a large collection,
not a handful of objects: objects, skins, characters, wallpapers, carpets and more, made with
SimBabes, SimFreaks, SimSlice and others — an authored world. The **Dumbold Voting Machine** is
exactly one object. The **tombstones** are two object prototypes, Halloween and Solemn, which
generated *thousands* of individual personalized stones. And **Transmogrifier** and **RugOMatic**
are not content at all — they are the tools that let anyone make any of it. Four different orders
of magnitude, which is itself the argument: the rhetoric scales from one object, to a collection,
to a generator, to a construction set.

## The chapter: the SimProv Wedding Playset

Design detail lives in [`../../../../catalogs/simprov/wedding-playset-depth.md`](../../../../catalogs/simprov/wedding-playset-depth.md).
What follows is the argument.

The Wedding Consultant does not open dialog boxes. She produces **documents** — in-world objects
that configure the simulation. A magazine you order services out of, where the officiant is a
parameter and the parameter lives in an ad: priest, rabbi, clown. An invitation list that works
like a phone which lets you toggle any household in the neighborhood instead of calling them one
at a time. A lawyer you can hire for a divorce, because an institution modeled honestly includes
its exits.

And a **marriage contract** that binds the two Sims and enables the ceremony. No license, no
wedding — the document is a capability token, not decoration.

**The contract binds two Sims. It does not check gender.**

That is the entire argument, and it is made in bureaucratic silence, on a form. Nobody announces
it. No cutscene congratulates you. No achievement fires. The paperwork simply does not contain
the question, and the ceremony proceeds. A game that celebrates your same-sex wedding has still
marked it as remarkable; a game whose county clerk never thought to ask has not.

### The timing was the statement

The playset dates to circa 2002. At that point same-sex marriage was legal in exactly one country
on earth — the Netherlands, since **1 April 2001** — and in **no** American state. Massachusetts
became the first on **17 May 2004**. Nationwide recognition arrived with *Obergefell* on
**26 June 2015**, thirteen years after the object shipped.

So the Wedding Playset issued marriage licenses to same-sex couples in a mass-market American
consumer product roughly two years before any American jurisdiction would, and it did so by
declining to write a single line of validation code. The statement wasn't a slogan attached to a
game. The statement *was* the schema.

### And it still runs

Don demoed the whole chain on video in 2019 —
[Simprov Wedding Play Set](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mwt5LJlrMe8), graphics by SimBabes and
SimFreaks, programming by Don Hopkins — and worked the license chain for two men on camera:
*"now Bob and Michael… male marriage — like, this is really hard."* Nineteen years after ship,
the paperwork still had no gender check, because there was never anything in there to remove.

That is the durability claim the paper makes about mechanism versus representation. A feature can
be patched out. A missing conditional cannot be patched out; you would have to add one.

### What was designed and never shipped

The pipeline was going to keep going: import your wedding Family Album and **generate custom
personalized content from the after-effects of the simulation** — paintings and blown-up
photographs of your own wedding to hang on your walls, a coffee-table storybook of the ceremony,
your play session crystallized into furniture and placed back into the world that produced it.
Personalized procedural content generated from social-simulation after-effects, designed circa
2002.

This matters to the second act of the paper. That unshipped pipeline is the direct ancestor of
what [Soul City](../../../../catalogs/soul-city/README.md) and Soul Angel revive: a simulation's
after-effects as publishable, personal, **portable** artifacts. The wedding was always going to
produce something you could keep. Now the thing you keep is the soul, and keeping it is how you
leave.

## Case two: the Dumbold Voting Machine

[The 2018 article, illustrated](../../../will-wright/sources/2018-04-24-dumbold-voting-machine/medium-article.md)

Built **just before the 2004 presidential election**. It looks like a working voting machine. It
is full of infuriating bugs, gaping security holes and malicious easter eggs that reproduce the
real failure modes of real electronic voting machines, plus illustrated pop-ups that teach the
actual issues, plus a Monkey mode featuring Baxter the trained chimpanzee election hacker.

Don's own description is the cleanest definition of procedural rhetoric written by someone who
didn't know the term: *"free speech and political commentary rendered as executable multimedia
software."* And the argument is not in the pop-ups — the pop-ups are the footnotes. The argument
is that **you can hack it**, easily, and that discovering this is fun. Play the object and you
arrive at "all election software should be open source and publicly inspectable" by yourself,
which is the only way anybody ever arrives anywhere.

### The juxtaposition the paper should not pass up

That election — **2 November 2004** — is the one in which voters in **eleven states** approved
constitutional amendments defining marriage as exclusively heterosexual, all by double-digit
margins, eight of them also barring civil unions, bringing the national total to seventeen states
with constitutional bans. Six months after Massachusetts.

So within about two years, on one platform, the same author shipped an object that quietly issued
same-sex marriage licenses and an object that taught you to distrust the machinery of the election
that constitutionalized the ban. These are not two hobbies. They are one argument about who gets
to write the rules, made twice, in the medium where rules are the thing you write.

## Case three: the tombstone generator

[The cemetery, reconstructed from the Archive](../../../ian-bogost/sources/original-sims-cemetery-epitaphs.md)

**Two object prototypes — Halloween and Solemn — and thousands of stones.** That ratio is the
whole design. A web generator let anyone engrave a personalized tombstone, upload a photograph of
the deceased, download it as a **working Sims object**, and email the link to friends. Two
templates; an unbounded number of memorials. The graveyard at originalsims.com grew from
**364 stones** (November 2004) to about **1,200** (September 2005) to **2,249** (June 2006).
Sextupled in nineteen months.

Don built the generator; the stones themselves carry SimSlice's object name, so the Halloween and
Solemn tombstone objects were Steve Alvey's.

**What it actually did: it made it possible to include dead people in the game.** Not represent
them, not commemorate them elsewhere — place them, with their photograph and their words, in a
lot you could walk through. The rhetoric is entirely about **who is allowed to author a
memorial**, and the answer the software gave was *anyone, right now, for free, with no
application.* No moderator, no verification, a download button. The two stones Don engraved
himself while testing his own software were **Dani Bunten Berry** — the trans game designer The
Sims 1 is dedicated to, in the credits, given a proper Solemn stone — and **Mark Weiser**, the
father of ubiquitous computing, memorialized inside a game about invisible computers embedded in
everyday objects.

The result, twenty years later, is the finding: in 2,249 tombstones written by strangers with no
moderation, **the worst thing in there is playground spite.** No slurs, no brigades, no doxxing.
People wrote each other's grief with care and with jokes. That is the counterfactual internet,
and it is still standing in the Archive with the photographs attached.

In some ways it is a **graveyard of the internet that was** — and the double meaning is exact.
It is a graveyard *made by* that internet, and it is now a graveyard *of* it. The stones
memorialize individual people; the site memorializes the conditions that let strangers be trusted
with an engraving tool. Both are buried in the same place, and the Archive is the only cemetery
either one has.

### The reading

**Do it in the game.** A best-of tombstone reading, on a lot, in The Sims 1: import a selection of
the real stones back in as working objects, walk the graveyard, and read them out loud. Sincere
grief, industry history engraved as it happened, jokes, an ice cream cone, "u smell."

It will be poignant in a way an essay cannot be, because the medium does the argument again: you
are standing in a game, in front of a stranger's grief, with their photograph on it, twenty years
after they typed it, and nobody vetted a word of it. Read the stones, then say what the reading
just demonstrated — the terms, the theory, the finding about spite being the ceiling. That is the
whole chapter delivered as a walk rather than a paragraph.

Segment lives with Ian Bogost's cemetery material and the death-and-zombies thread; the object
import path is the Sims 1 Soul Bridge.

Which makes the cemetery evidence for the same claim as the wedding contract, from the opposite
direction: give people a construction set and no gatekeeper, and the thing they build is mostly
kind. The platforms that came after assumed the opposite and built accordingly.

## The tools: Transmogrifier and RugOMatic

Yes, both — and they make a *stronger* argument than the objects do, because a tool argues about
who gets to make the arguments.

Frasca's four ideological levels of a game put representations at the bottom and **meta-rules** at
the top — editors, modding, source access. That is the level where a tool operates. An object
persuades the player who installs it. A tool persuades by **redistributing authorship**, and its
claim is not about weddings or voting machines but about the medium itself: *this game's contents
are yours to write.*

### Transmogrifier — the claim that the catalog is editable

[License and distribution history](../../career/contracts/transmogrifier-distribution-license-notes.md)
· [1996–2004 renovation plan](../../../will-wright/sources/2004-02-05-transmogrifier-renovation-plan/article.md)

Clone any object in the game, open it up, change its graphics and its behavior, and ship the
result to strangers. EA granted Don permission to distribute the Windows binary under a
click-through license; he was the authorized distributor, not the owner of the assets it
manipulated.

The argument it executes: **a shipped catalog is a starting condition, not a boundary.** Nothing
in the game says that. The tool says it by working. And note what this does to the inclusivity
thesis — every community object in this chapter, and most of the twenty-six years of Sims content
the paper leans on, exists downstream of somebody being handed an editor. Inclusivity that
survives is inclusivity somebody else can author. Sihvonen's modding argument is this paragraph
with citations.

### RugOMatic — illustrated blog posts you put on the floor

A rug is an image plus a title plus a description. You make one, you place it on the floor, and
the title and description show up in the catalog and in the popup when a Sim or the player looks
at it.

That is a **blog post**. Image, headline, body text, published to a place where visitors encounter
it — and the place is your living room floor, so the encounter is *ambient* rather than
navigational. Nobody browses to it. They walk over it. Carpets as pictures plus narration,
underfoot, in the room where the story happens.

Which makes it the closest thing in the chapter to a general-purpose publishing medium, and it
predates most of what we now call one. The
[RugOMatic picture rig](../../../../catalogs/soul-city/object-shops.md) in the Soul City catalog
takes the obvious next step: one rug holding many images, switchable from the pie menu, with a
Family Album imported into it — walk on your own story.

And then the part that makes it rhetoric rather than a feature: **the rug is flammable.** Don set
one on fire by accident during his own Simplifier demo on video, and Sims died horrible deaths on
top of the illustrated blog post. The publishing surface is a physical object in a simulation with
fire propagation and mortality, so your writing shares a world with consequences. You cannot say
that about a webpage. The medium's argument is that publishing here is *situated* — it occupies
space, it obstructs pathfinding, it burns.

## Why all of this belongs in the paper

The paper's thesis is that inclusivity in The Sims was **mechanism, not decoration**, and that
mechanism is what made it durable. These are that argument in miniature, and they share a shape:


| What | Scale | The argument | Where it lives |
| ---- | ----- | ------------ | -------------- |
| SimProv Wedding Playset | A collection — objects, skins, characters, wallpapers, carpets | Same-sex marriage is unremarkable | A contract with no gender check |
| Dumbold Voting Machine | One object | Election software must be inspectable | Failure modes you enjoy discovering |
| Tombstone generator | Two prototypes, thousands of stones | Anyone may author a memorial, including for the dead | A free download button and no moderator |
| Transmogrifier | A tool | The shipped catalog is a starting condition, not a boundary | It works, therefore the claim is true |
| RugOMatic | A tool | Publishing can be situated, ambient, and flammable | Image plus title plus description, on the floor |

None of them states its argument. Each one *executes* it, which is why players arrive at the
conclusion as their own. All of it predates Bogost naming the mechanism in *Persuasive Games*
(2007). The two tools sit at Frasca's meta-rule level, which is why they generalize: an object
persuades whoever installs it, and a tool persuades by handing over the authorship.

## And still — right now

The Wedding Playset made its statement when same-sex marriage was illegal everywhere its players
lived. It is making one again. Under the reported change of ownership at EA, the queer systems in
the current game are patchable features sitting in a character creator: the pronouns, the
orientation setting, the binders, the top surgery scars. All addable, therefore all removable.

The 2002 contract is not removable, because the thing that makes it inclusive is an absence.
That is not a rhetorical flourish, it's a design lesson, and it's the one the second act of this
paper is built on: **build the argument into the schema, and put the exit in the player's hands.**
A soul you can carry out is the 2026 version of a license nobody thought to gender-check.

Inclusivity saved The Sims once by making better rules. The rules are still the only part that
holds.
