# Jokes and their Relation to the Cognitive Unconscious

Marvin Minsky · AI Memo 603, MIT AI Lab, November 1980 · published in *Cognitive
Constraints on Communication* (Vaina & Hintikka eds., Reidel, 1981)

This directory is the front door to everything we have on the paper: what it says, where
to read it, and where it works for a living.

## The title is the thesis

In 1905 Freud published *Der Witz und seine Beziehung zum Unbewußten* — in English,
**"Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious."** Minsky's title is Freud's title with one
word inserted. That word is the whole argument.

Freud said the mind has **censors** — unconscious barriers that keep forbidden thoughts
(sex, aggression) from surfacing. A joke smuggles the forbidden thought past the childishly
literal censor in disguise; laughter is the leak. It worked beautifully for dirty jokes and
cruel ones, and Freud knew it failed for **nonsense** — he conceded "we do not yet know in
what the characteristic of being a joke resides," and he never solved it.

Minsky's move, seventy-five years later: keep the censors, **extend their jurisdiction**.
The mind doesn't only censor forbidden *wishes* — it censors defective *reasoning*.
Common-sense logic is unreliable and unrepairable, so each of us builds a private
collection of censors that recognize the precursors of known thought-bugs: paradoxes,
circular exchanges, frame confusions, false analogies. Nonsense jokes are how those
censors get trained. The cognitive unconscious "considers faulty reasoning to be just as
naughty as the usual Freudian wishes" — and with that, the two kinds of humor Freud
couldn't unify become one mechanism.

Laughter, in this theory, does two jobs at once: it **disrupts** (stops you from following
the absurd thought any further, from taking it seriously) and it **focuses** (holds the bug
up in short-term memory so the censor-builder can learn its shape). Which explains the
oldest puzzle in comedy: why a joke dies on second hearing. The censor already took the
class. And why the forbidden-subject jokes stay funny for decades — those particular
censors, Minsky suggests, are the slow learners of the mind.

One sentence in the paper is of special interest to anyone who writes comedy for a living:

> The element that seems to me most common to all the different kinds of humor is that of
> unexpected frame-substitution, in which a scene is first described from one viewpoint and
> then suddenly — **typically by a single word** — one is made to view all the
> scene-elements in another, quite different way.

(Emphasis ours.) That single word is what comedians call the punch word. Minsky is describing, from inside
the head, the same mechanism working comics engineer from outside.

He also settles a score with Freud directly. In Note 11 he catches Freud botching his own
spinach/mayonnaise joke — retelling it with the wrong absurdity in the wrong slot and
spoiling the frame-shift — and deadpans that he "would not mention this were it not for the
established tradition of advancing psychiatry by analyzing Freud's own writings."

His closing line: **"jokes are not really funny at all, but reflect the most serious of
concerns; the pursuit of sobriety through the suppression of the absurd."**
