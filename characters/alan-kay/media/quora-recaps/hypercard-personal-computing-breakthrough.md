## Personal computing's ARPA definition — symmetric authoring

"Personal Computing" and "Personal Computers" were defined by the **ARPA-IPTO/PARC community** (60s–70s, roots
to Whirlwind ~1950s): interactive **"symmetric use and authoring" of "everything"** — end-users should do what
professionals do, plus things professionals hadn't thought of or devalued. That requires serious work on
**end-user programming**.

Early exemplars (~1962): **Sketchpad** (Sutherland), **LINC** (Wes Clark), **JOSS** (Cliff Shaw at RAND — first
great end-user programming system). **Engelbart's** 1962 proposal became a central "what are we trying to do?"
resource.

PARC added **media and meta-media** (Dynabook / flat bitmap display): GUI, fonts, DTP, 2.5D animation, end-user
programming in Smalltalk by adults and children — programming the media, not just using it. Mac and Windows
were **subsets** on weaker hardware.

## Bill Atkinson's line — Magic Slate → Rolodex → Wild Card → HyperCard

**Bill Atkinson** (Apple Fellow) wanted a Dynabook-like **"Magic Slate"**: a stack of pages, screen shows top,
instant search to any page; pages hold any media.

1984 Mac: **"Rolodex"** demo — text on simulated cards, instant search faster than hitting "next"; insight:
**retrieval over-retrieves** → need fastest **rejection**. Used for everything.

Magic Slate became **"Wild Card"** for Mac; trademark conflict → **"HyperCard"** (1987).

Internal demo had features but **not end-user scripting**. Apple marketing didn't want to ship it. Kay (also
Fellow) helped Bill; they got **John Sculley** to champion it. Kay insisted it be **"end-user programmable"**
— Sculley-blessed productization was "immense" and "left nothing to chance." Kay stayed away enough not to
"ruin" it by over-insisting — result: **"masterpiece"** (with things not done right, but masterpiece was the
point).

## The adoption numbers — still unmatched

HyperCard was a **"complete breakthrough"** as wide-spectrum end-user personal computing — "brilliant naivete"
made a **small part** of PARC's vision **more learnable and useful** for average users than PARC's own systems.

Within a few years Apple counted **>4 million scripted applications** by different users in **HyperTalk** — Kay
believes this remains the **largest successful wide-scale adoption** of an end-user media system with real
programming in history.

*(Kay's longer answer analyzes HyperTalk's design: dynamic context layers scripts attach to; card/stack
background hierarchy; readable English-like syntax; ad hocness that felt nice at first vs scaling problems;
Tony Hoare-style remark that HyperCard was great "for its time" and also for all time in some dimensions.)*

## What Apple and the web failed to do next

In the **"Has the real computer revolution happened yet?"** thread (see
[`computer-revolution-not-yet.md`](computer-revolution-not-yet.md)), Kay notes the **web and browser came years
after HyperCard** — yet there's no "equivalent of HyperCard in 2020." Apple counted 4M+ user scripts then
**dropped HyperCard** when they "didn't understand" it.

Flash and others carried HyperTalk-influenced scripting; Kay argues sensibilities for great end-user authoring
(HyperCard, ARPA vision) were **not** what drove the web — browsers went document-viewer/subset route instead.

Kay's regret in the browsers thread (cross-ref): the NeWS/HyperLook crowd **"missed the significance … of
Hypertalk"** — end-users seeing their own faces in the same terms they read.
