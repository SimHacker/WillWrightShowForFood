## His question

> Thank you Ted, this has been very exciting to hear. I'm curious about your take on Scrivener and the
> failings that you sort of observe with it. Are they to do with the fact that it's sort of fairly
> opinionated about fiction writing, that it's unapproachable for what you would like to do with it, or

reading that parses. Marked rather than silently fixed.

## Ted's answer — the "I'm lazy" quote, in full

> Wasn't aware that you could write hypertexts in Scrivener. Basically I was simply daunted by its
> complexity. **I'm lazy, and not eager to spend hours delving into the complexities of a particular
> piece of software.**

Earlier in the same talk, on what he actually uses:

> The tool I use is scissors and a stapler in the outsy position.

And on Scrivener specifically, before the Q&A: it has rearrangement, but it is too complex for him;
his son likes it.

## Why this exchange is the design brief

Two sentences, from two people, one minute apart, that state the whole problem:

1. **Brandel:** hypertext authoring is valuable, and it may be too complex to be relevant to most
   people.
2. **Ted:** I'm lazy and I will not spend hours learning your software.

The man who coined *hypertext*, who has been trying to build the tool since the 1960s, who does
"full noodle" revision with physical scissors every day — bounced off a shipping hypertext writing
tool because it was too much work to learn. That is not a knock on Ted. It is the acceptance test.

Anything the webtop asks a reader or author to learn has to survive that test, and
[`designs/SONG-FORM.md`](https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/SONG-FORM.md) is the
current answer: explain structure as music, because everyone already reads it and nobody studied it.

## Context he was asking in

Immediately after this exchange, Selker steered Ted to his books, which produced the bibliography beat:
*The Scene Machine* (a hybrid computer for making Pixar-style movies), *Media 72* (a 1967 enumeration
of coming media innovations, written for his boss at Harcourt Brace), *Possiblex* — an autobiography
whose separate threads are set in different fonts — and *Philosophy of Hypertext*, his PhD thesis from
Keio University, based on a philosophy of "general schematics" he dates to 1957. All via lulu.com.

The *Possiblex* detail is worth keeping: parallel threads distinguished by typeface, because
single-column text could not hold them. He then says he likes fonts as much as the next person and is
sorry about what they stuck us with.
