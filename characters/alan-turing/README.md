## The other founding memorial

If [John von Neumann](../john-von-neumann/) designed a machine that could *copy itself*, Turing
designed the machine that could *be* any other machine -- and then, in the last years of a
short life, turned the same originality on biology. Alan Kay puts Turing first on his "magic"
list: "they did something important to advance the field, and it is difficult to see just how
they were able to do it given when they did it and what they were starting with"
([Quora recap](../alan-kay/media/quora-recaps/magic-category-and-the-good-great-line.md)).

The universal machine arrived as a byproduct of answering Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem. The
computer followed. The morphogenesis work -- one published paper, a drawer of unfinished
manuscripts -- is the part that still surprises people who think they already know the story.

## Hockey or Watching the Daisies Grow

Inside the front cover of Don's copy of *Collected Works of A.M. Turing, Volume 3:
Morphogenesis* (P.T. Saunders, ed., 1992) is a drawing by Turing's mother of her son on the
school hockey field in the Springtime of 1923. The caption is *Hockey or Watching the Daisies
Grow*. Don scanned it:

![Hockey or Watching the Daisies Grow -- drawing by Ethel Sara Turing, 1923](media/hockey-or-watching-the-daisies-grow.jpg)

He also typed in the volume's prefatory texts -- Furbank's preface, the chronology, Saunders's
preface to the volume, and Saunders's introduction -- because they are the best short portrait
of Turing-as-biologist, and because they contain the line that belongs next to von Neumann's
three constructors:

> his new ideas were "intended to defeat the argument from design"

([Hodges 1983, p. 431](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing:_The_Enigma), quoted by
Saunders.) Turing, following D'Arcy Thompson, tried to show that the arrangement of leaves
is a natural consequence of the process that produces them -- form from physics and chemistry,
not from a watchmaker, blind or otherwise. He did not finish phyllotaxis. The
reaction-diffusion model survived him; Turing structures have been observed experimentally.

## Why this sits next to von Neumann

Saunders's introduction records the road Turing *didn't* take: given the Turing machine and
Colossus, he might have seen development as a program in the genes, or invented something like
what we now call cellular automata -- "models in which the fate of a cell is determined by the
states of its neighbours through some simple algorithm, in a way that is very reminiscent of
the Turing machine." He went to physics and chemistry instead. Von Neumann, in the same decade,
went to the grid of cells. Two answers to the same question -- how does a simple local rule
make a body? -- filed in two memorial rooms that should always be read as a pair.

## Show hooks

- Kay's magic criterion applied strictly: Turing is the first name, and the criterion is
  almost a description of 1936.
- Morphogenesis as the missing chapter in every "Turing invented the computer" story -- and
  the daisy drawing as the image that belongs on the wall.
- "Defeat the argument from design" next to von Neumann's unfinished probabilistic constructor:
  two 1950s attempts to put evolution inside a machine, one chemical, one cellular.
- Church of the Eval Genius adjacency: Turing at Princeton with Alonzo Church, 1936-38 -- see
  the [magic recap Church paragraph](../alan-kay/media/quora-recaps/magic-category-and-the-good-great-line.md)
