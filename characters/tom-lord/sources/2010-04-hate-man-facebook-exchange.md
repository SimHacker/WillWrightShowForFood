# "Hate man made the news" — Tom and Don on Facebook, April 2010

Tom's own testimony, in his own words, about Hate Man and the brothers of People's Park saving
his life. Primary source: screenshot of the Facebook exchange, preserved at
[`../media/2010-04-hate-man-facebook-exchange.png`](../media/2010-04-hate-man-facebook-exchange.png).
Quoted verbatim below, typos and all. This exchange is the heart of Don's July 2026 HN
remembrance and of the "I HATE YOU!!!!" post to Tom's memorial page.

## Context

Hate Man was Mark Hawthorne (1936–2017), a New York Times reporter from 1961 to 1970 who opted
out of normal society and lived for decades on the streets around Telegraph Avenue and People's
Park, requiring anyone who wanted to talk to him to first say "I hate you" — his philosophy of
being straight about negative feelings so a real conversation can happen.

Homeless ex-reporter opted for Berkeley streets, Kevin Fagan, SF Chronicle, April 11, 2010 (archived):

https://web.archive.org/web/20100413124819/http://www.sfgate.com/cgi-bin/article.cgi?f=/c/a/2010/04/11/BA831COQP3.DTL

Key Hate Man quotes from the article:

> It's a new way of hating. It's about being straight with people. The dictionary defines hate
> as hostility, but that's heavy. My idea is to be straight about negative feelings that we all
> have, which is what hate is, and then you can have a real conversation.

> Love? There's nothing wrong with that. I'm into positives, but we should be straight about the
> negatives to clear them first. My philosophy is to feel the positives more than say them. Act
> them out.

## The exchange (verbatim)

**Thomas Lord:** That reminds me (why? I dunno) that - hey: hate man made the news:
http://www.sfgate.com/cgi-bin/article.cgi?f=/c/a/2010/04/11/BA831COQP3.DTL

**Don Hopkins:** I love hate man!
Oh wait, that kinda sounds weird, like I totally missed the point.
But I'm honored that he hated on me once. I wish we had discussed it more deeply.
He seems like one of the most sensible people walking around Berkeley.
He's much happier and has his shit together so much more than the angry old homeless Republican
Wingnut-job with the "Anita Hill Lies" bumper sticker on his shopping basket that he pushes
around Berkeley while listening to Rush Limbaugh on his AM radio.

**Thomas Lord:** You said it, man. Back in the day I had some really, really bad days - real
existential crises, if you know what I mean. Hate man and several of the brothers in the park
basically saved my life. Hate man by getting me to play his "let's lean on each other game" and
the brothers just by being good listeners to mumbled words and often enough good council. Oh,
and also (the brothers I mean) by whooping my ass at street chess. After that I noticed that --
well, back then Telegraph was still very much the go to spot for itinerant run-away teens sans a
roof over their head. I guess it still is, a bit, but back then it was so "quite a bit". As
things shut down for the night he'd gather the rough punks up in a circle in Sproul plaza (maybe
still does, for all I know). They'd all chill and keep safe and share warmth and make peace -
all in the name of Hate. Telegraph was very much a zen temple. Hate man a famous monk.

The only thing wrong with the Chronicle's article there is that they place the start of his
street life in '86. Well, bull shit. Hate man was the first resident of Berkeley I ever heard
of, as such. In '83. In Massachusetts. From someone who was basically trying to encourage me to
run as fast as I could away from the preppy high school that G. W. Bush went to; suggesting
Berkeley as a good direction to head. "There's this guy there. And he's really cool. He likes to
shout 'I hate you' at people. And he won't talk to you unless you first tell him that you hate
him, too." It made no sense to me at the time.

Between Hate man and a few others, arriving in Berkeley was, for me, a bit like walking into the
landscape of a favorite children's book I'd read years before. Many of the familiar characters
were there. Even so, it took a fair amount of time to be able to see them as real people.

**Don Hopkins:** That's beautiful!

**Thomas Lord:** I hate you.

**Don Hopkins:** Go stick your head in a pig, beeeyatch!

in the conversation again: **I HATE YOU!!!!**

## Why this matters for the room

- Tom's own voice, verbatim, about the darkest days and what saved him — People's Park "held
  great meaning for him" (obituary) is abstract; this is the concrete testimony behind it.
- The '83 Massachusetts story: Berkeley as a children's book landscape he walked into. His
  origin myth, told by him.
- Oppositionality as the grammar of Tom and Don's friendship: heatedly arguing while strongly
  agreeing. The argument is the handshake.
