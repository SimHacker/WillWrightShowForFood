# In memoriam — Tom Lord (1966–2022)

*Narrative tribute — not the room index. Front page: [README.md](README.md).*

*Memorial mode: we represent and discuss his work and the stories friends tell; we do not interview him or speak as him.*
[Memorial standards](../../schemas/portrayal-standards.md#memorial-mode)

Tom Lord created **Guile**. Not the compiler it became, but the thing itself: in 1993 at Cygnus he
forked Aubrey Jaffer's SCM into an embeddable library he called **GEL**, and talked RMS into
blessing it as the official GNU extension language. The rename to Guile came after trademark
trouble. The idea he was arguing for — one general Scheme engine underneath, other languages and
applications on top — was grander than the resources of its day, and it took decades and other
hands (Andy Wingo's compiler, VM, and JIT; Guix as the killer app) to vindicate it. But it was
vindicated.

He was at the center of the **Tcl War of 1994**, one of free software's formative flamewars, and
he wrote its definitive insider history: Sun swaggering that Tcl would be "the ubiquitous
scripting language of the Internet," his Scheme-based GDB GUI project skunked by a quick Tcl/Tk
one, his venting to RMS, and "Why you should not use Tcl" landing on comp.lang.tcl with
deliberately murky authorship he would only ever call a semi-prank by "the Scheme underground."
His account is him on the page: brilliant, funny, hard on himself, and fair even to the people
who did him wrong.

An Account of the Tcl War, by Thomas Lord:

https://web.archive.org/web/20110102015130/http://basiscraft.com/0800-0100-the-tcl-war.html

In 2001 he started **GNU arch** (`tla` — Tom Lord's Arch), one of the earliest distributed
version control systems, years before git. The argument arch made — that version control should
be distributed — won completely, even though arch itself did not.

**Don knew him for decades.** They argued the way you can only argue with someone who has
actually built the thing being argued about: Don shipping SimCity on Tcl/Tk while Tom fought
the war against it, XML versus S-expressions in 1998 with RMS cc'd, Self and V8 and the lost
art of compilable language design in 2006. Tom's body of work and his philosophical posts about
extension languages, universal engines, and who gets to control the substrate are a rich source
of wisdom that hardly anybody mines.

Berkeley wasn't just where he lived; by his own account it saved him. In a 2010 conversation
with Don he wrote that in his darkest days, "Hate man and several of the brothers in the park
basically saved my life" — Hate Man being Mark Hawthorne, the ex-New York Times reporter monk
of Telegraph Avenue who wouldn't talk to you until you told him you hated him, being straight
about the negatives so a real conversation could happen. Tom first heard of him in 1983 in
Massachusetts, and said arriving in Berkeley was like walking into the landscape of a favorite
children's book he'd read years before. The full exchange, in his own words:

[Tom and Don on Hate Man, April 2010](sources/2010-04-hate-man-facebook-exchange.md)

In Berkeley, where he settled permanently in 2004, he brought the same fierce, principled
intensity to civic life: contributing to the Berkeley Daily Planet, serving on the City Housing
Advisory Commission, advocating for housing and the homeless, and — after reading the IPCC's
SR15 report and following Greta Thunberg's work — speaking the truth about the climate emergency
and demanding it be treated as an actual emergency. He volunteered coaching young writers at
Longfellow Middle School. People's Park held great meaning for him. He made his case in public,
at full strength, to the end. He died unexpectedly of a massive brain hemorrhage in June 2022,
at 56.
