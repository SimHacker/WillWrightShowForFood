## HCI = human-CONSTRUCT interaction

> The computer has no nature not even hierarchy. What people interact with is the constructs we enact on the computer.

Evil constructs named in this talk:
1. **The abominable clipboard** (invisible buffer)
2. **Single-column text** (after clipboard — "wrongful construct number two")
3. Renamed **cut and paste** → hide and plug

## Cut and paste — the opening (paste pots)

**NY Times copy boy (1960s):** First morning task — scrape dried paste from glass pots, refill with wet library paste (three on city desk, foreign, economics). Reporters cut sections, paste down, rewrite between. Yell *copy* → pneumatic tube to typesetting. *"This was called cut and paste and indeed it was."*

**Grandmother's Tolstoy story:** Daughter's lecture — Tolstoy dictates two longhand copies; one filed, one **cut up all over the dacha floor**; walking in woods: *"Don't touch my noodles"*; returns to paste in new order and dictate next draft.

**Slide (Ted Selker showed):** Ted's 1965 ACM article noodles — bad reproduction of actual cutting/pasting with photo of real noodles.

## Clipboard rant (core)

> Those holy words referring to the highest faculties of an author's overview have been assigned to a strange constructor called the clipboard an **invisible buffer** you can't see it and it holds only one object in other any other respects it is like a physical clipboard **except there aren't any other respects**.

> These features should have been called **hide and plug** but somehow using those familiar old words many signs sound okay like the words **national socialism**.

> They could at least have made it visible and made it a stack for rearrangement but oh no.

> This **crime against humanity** … was perpetrated by a good friend of mine the late protector **Larry Tesler** — many emails; he insisted real cut and paste was only for specialist long-form work.

**Ted's tool today:** *"The tool I use is scissors and a stapler in the outsy position."* Still does **full noodle** — print, cut up, transfer order to word processor. Scrivener has rearrangement but too complex; son likes it.

### Suyash: can this visualize code? — and who actually answered

Corrects an earlier version of this digest, which credited the Interlisp-D answer to Ted. It was
Selker's. Ted's own answer is the more interesting one for MOOLLM.

The question, on code that "is calling something else and something else, but there is no tool that
kind of visualizes in an intuitive way." **Ted:**

> My answer is, I don't know. Certainly automatic understanding of computer code is hard. That's why
> documentation of the computer programs used to be so important -- it probably still is -- and that's
> by **the author actually putting it into the words**.

**Then Selker**, unprompted, with the history:

> Interlisp-D with LOOPS was a programming environment that made nodes for all the different objects,
> and embedded all of the code inside of those nodes, and used structure editors -- and it literally
> changed the style of programming for the people that used it. Smalltalk also had a visual metaphor
> for its tools that changed the way people wrote their code. They made much deeper code -- anyway,
> more balanced code -- in LOOPS. There's a whole field of people using visualization. Mike Travers, on
> our talk, has been involved with this as well. It's a very very important topic you just brought up,
> and it's a field, so it's an interesting field too.

("Interlisp-D" is rendered "interlift d" in the captions.) Both halves are used in
https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/STRUCTURE-EDITORS.md

### Nicole Lazzaro: the serial port

> One of my challenges is often I'll have three ideas in my head at the same time, and then I have a
> **serial port**, like which is a mouse, or hands which is typing, and I can't get all three ideas
> out. So hypertext is kind of an interesting way around that, if you will. But I was curious how does
> that reflect at all about your inner workings, like how you think, how you create.

**Ted**, complete answer:

> I don't know. It seemed an obvious idea to me when I published it in 1965.

### Nicole Lazzaro: mind mapping

**Ted:**

> I've looked at it and fiddled with it and didn't find any use for it. Hypertext I see is the
> extension of literature, especially parallel hypertext side by side. But mind mapping doesn't create
> -- okay, a document is a package of information with a point of view, and it has to be portable, and
> that has to be extendable to somebody else. And mind maps don't seem to fill a bill.

Her reply: *"the mind map in terms of like thebrain.com is often might be just a little bit too
atomized in a way."* Ted: *"I love that distinction."*

### Aaron Marcus: MCC, the puppet, and the through-line

Aaron opens with **"and indeed you used to carry a puppet around as I recall"** — Ted: **"uh, it's
upstairs."** (Aaron remembered it; the puppet is Ted's and he still has it.)

Aaron consulted at MCC in Austin 1985-90, where "the cognitive engineering people were trying to find
ways to visualize thought processes," and asks whether Ted ever saw their 3D work, since it resembled
his n-dimensional lego blocks. Ted recalls being invited to speak at MCC — by someone the captions
render "eric bullockson", **unresolved, do not guess** — says "I was in San Antonio" (he was at
Datapoint), and does not recall seeing the work. Then, unprompted:

> the issue about document structure is it has to be something that can be sent to another person,
> and it has to express a point of view. A person has to make it, because it has overview -- and or it
> doesn't. You know, it can be badly written or well written. The term **through line** I only learned
> in recent years, but you know, in through line writing you start with a zinger and end by coming back
> to that zinger, and that's how many New Yorker type articles are written.

Load-bearing in https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/PAIRED-LINKS.md

### Mike Travers: itty bitty machine company

### ZigZag, in Ted's own words

> I actually designed it. It's a system of **lego blocks and n dimensions**, each block has a positive
> and negative end in each dimension, and you can connect them into remarkable structures.

He told Marlene the idea when they first met; **she dreamt about it and loved it**. Built 2001 in
Finland at Jyväskylä ("ubascala" in the captions) under **Tuomas J. Lukka** — "they did a perfect job
of everything I asked for." **Adam Moore** (Nottingham) populated the British kings and queens demo,
then the chemical structures demo shown in the talk. Ted's "third big mistake" was patenting ZigZag in
the USA.
