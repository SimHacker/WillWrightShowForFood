## Ben on Ted

Don had just read Ben Shneiderman's influences list and agreed with both the praise and the criticism.

> Ted Nelson’s clever and innovative Computer Lib (1974) book and other writings demonstrated what truly innovative thinking was like. I’ve had the chance to meet Ted occasionally and am constantly impressed by his innovative thinking, but I am among those who wish he would link himself more closely to practical realities. Maybe that is too pedestrian of me, but it reflects my desire to be innovative while also having an impact.

— Ben Shneiderman, [Eager Eyes](https://eagereyes.org/influences/ben-shneiderman)

[*Computer Lib / Dream Machines*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Lib/Dream_Machines) (1974), two front covers, "intertwingled." Self-published, then Microsoft Press in 1987 with a foreword by Stewart Brand. Room: [`../../stewart-brand/`](../../stewart-brand/).

## HyperTIES, which Ben is good at shipping

Don's contrast: Ben explains ideas from user tests and gets them into the world. The light-blue link color is the example. Berners-Lee's Spring 1989 proposal cites the July 1988 *Communications of the ACM* — *Hypertext on Hypertext*, Hyperties, Cognetics — as the source of hot spots, and uses that blue. Embedded menus: Shneiderman and Dan Ostroff, 1983; IJMMS January 1986; CACM April 1986. *Hypertext Hands-On!* with Greg Kearsley, 1988, the first electronic book, same system.

Ben's own account: http://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/about.html
Berners-Lee: http://www.w3.org/History/1989/proposal.html
Hyperlink history: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperlink#History

Nelson, 1964–1980, took Bush's cross-reference, applied it to strings rather than pages, and scaled it to a proprietary worldwide network. Autodesk funded Xanadu in the 1980s. It did not build that network. Engelbart's NLS (Jeff Rulifson, chief programmer) had the hyperlink in a single document in 1966 and between documents in 1968.

## The 1999 source drop

Don's longer writeup of the open-source release is the continuation he points at: [HN 16224154](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=16224154). The claim, in his words from this comment: Xanadu was written in Smalltalk, then run through a Smalltalk-to-C++ compiler, and what they released was the compiler's output — unreadable, and not something anyone could improve. Open-sourcing something that was never going to ship, in 1999, when open source was the fad. [The Register](http://www.theregister.co.uk/1999/08/27/web_precursor_xanadu) notes Microsoft Press had published *Computer Lib*.
