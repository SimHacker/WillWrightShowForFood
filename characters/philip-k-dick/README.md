## Who he was

Philip K. Dick was born in Chicago in 1928 and lived most of his life in California, writing fast
and for little money: 44 novels and over a hundred stories. His recurring questions were what is
real and what is human, and his settings are full of machines that have opinions.

In *Ubik* (1969), the world of the dead-but-not-quite decays around the characters, and the only
thing that holds it together is a product sold in a spray can; doors and appliances demand coins
before they will work. In *The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch* (1965), colonists on Mars escape
into Perky Pat layouts, miniature worlds of dolls and furniture they inhabit by drug. *Do Androids
Dream of Electric Sheep?* (1968) asks how to tell an android from a person by testing for empathy.
"Autofac" (1955) imagines automated factories that keep building after people want them to stop,
and "We Can Remember It for You Wholesale" (1966) sells implanted memories.

In February and March 1974 he had experiences he spent the rest of his life trying to explain, in
*VALIS* (1981) and in thousands of pages of notes published in 2011 as *The Exegesis*. In 1977 in
Metz he gave a talk proposing that we may live in one of many programmed worlds. He died in 1982,
months before *Blade Runner* opened.

## Why he's here

His books read like design documents for the things this project builds: simulated people in
dollhouse worlds, machines with personalities, and systems that run on after their makers lose
control. Mark Weiser told Don that *Ubik* was one of his inspirations for ubiquitous computing.
The [reading group](../mark-weiser/pkd-lem-ai-sf.md) reads Dick beside Stanisław Lem, and MOOLLM
has [design notes](https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/tree/main/designs/pkd) on *Ubik*, Perky Pat
and *A Scanner Darkly*.
