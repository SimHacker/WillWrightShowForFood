# PIXIE History — Back to the Roots (Part 1, 1967)

*By **Heinz U. Lemke**, written March 2020.* His own account, in his own words —
lightly formatted, not paraphrased. Sent 9 July 2026 to the PIXIE Repo Show thread
as the first installment of the Cambridge storyline
[Roy Eagleson asked for](2026-07-09-pixie-storyline-thread.md). More parts to
follow. [Portrayal standards](../../../schemas/portrayal-standards.md) — Heinz may
correct, shape, or remove any of it.

---

Some of the roots of PIXIE can be traced back 53 years, to **March 1967**, when
Heinz worked as a mathematician at the **Plessey Automation Research Laboratories**
in Poole, Dorset, UK.

One morning that month his boss sent him — with no explanation — straight to the
director of research, **Dr. Phil Cook**. In Heinz's words:

> *"[I] was received by a serious looking gentleman who quickly came to the point
> by letting me know, that there is a report by the librarian of the lab, who
> suspects me of being a German spy. What is my answer to this accusation?"*

The evidence against him: he worked not only 9-to-5 but also **5pm to 10pm in the
company library** — "the unusual (and suspicious!) hours" — pursuing his real
interest, **computer modelling and interaction with those models for electronic
circuit design**. That, plus being a conscientious objector to German army service
and living in England by personal choice (this only 22 years after the war), was
enough. Cook believed him, and asked him to write up his thinking on computing.
Heinz produced **7 handwritten pages, dated 10 March 1967**.

Then the turn that made everything else possible. Cook took Heinz on a day trip to
Cambridge to meet a professor he knew personally: **Prof. Maurice Wilkes**. Wilkes
was receptive to the rough ideas — and immediately insisted Heinz give a talk at
the **Cambridge Mathematical Laboratory**, in the regular Wednesday-afternoon
lecture slot (with the tea reception after).

Heinz had **never given a talk in his life**. He negotiated a three-month delay and
was scheduled for **29 June 1967** *(attachment 1 — the announcement and list of
participants, still to arrive)*. He prepared hard, travelling to Cambridge and
rehearsing late evenings in the lecture hall of the Arts School "to an imaginative
audience."

He didn't know until later that Wilkes had **invited colleagues from all over the
UK**. He got through the talk and the Q&A anyway. Then, after the tea break, Wilkes
took him aside and asked whether he'd like to do a **PhD in Cambridge in Interactive
Computer Graphics** on what he'd just presented. Heinz explained he had **never been
to university** and had **no funding**. Wilkes was unbothered: as a member of the
Cambridge Academic Senate he knew of **a 13th- or 14th-century statute** permitting
exactly such exceptions — and he convinced Plessey's Phil Cook it was a good
investment for the company too.

Heinz started at Cambridge — *"probably the most decisive part of my life"* — on
**1 October 1967**, under the supervision of **Dr. Neil Wiseman**. Plessey raised his
salary, covered the fees, and expected him back in three years with a graphic
communication system "in my pocket." By the end of 1967 Wiseman set him the task of
writing a single-authored paper on the modelling side; to his own surprise it was
accepted for the **C.A.C.D. Conference, early 1968** [1]. His earlier two years as a
**technical draftsman in Germany** gave him a feel for drawing technical artefacts
ergonomically — now on a screen instead of a board. *Writing* about it, he says, was
"an entirely different cup of tea" — and how to write well has been a preoccupation
ever since.

That support from Wilkes and Wiseman was the foundation of three years of **PIXIE**
development — the joint 1969 paper with Wiseman and Hiles [2], and the two films,
*Interaction **with** PIXIE* (1969) and *Interaction **using** PIXIE* (1970) [3, 4].
The second film became teaching material and effectively summarized his thesis; the
final PhD thesis was submitted in **1972** [5].

Before leaving Cambridge in **December 1970** to return to Plessey, Heinz summarized
his thesis work in a seminar for the Cambridge research community — Wilkes and
Wiseman present — again on a Wednesday afternoon *(attachment 2, still to arrive)*.
His closing line:

> *"By the way, Maurice Wilkes and Neil Wiseman did not mind that I typically worked
> all night in the Mathematical Laboratory, and never accused me of being a German
> spy."*

## Why this matters here

This is the human root of everything the [PIXIE show](../../../repo-shows/pixie-pie-menus-pdp7.yml)
is trying to preserve — and it settles a few facts from Heinz's own hand: the
authoritative **PIXIE paper citation** (Wiseman/Lemke/Hiles, I.E.E. International CAD
Conference, Southampton, **1969**), the **1972** thesis, and the exact roles of
**Maurice Wilkes** (who created the opening) and **Neil Wiseman** (who supervised the
work). It also answers Roy's half-joke directly: if anyone in this cast deserves a
SIMs avatar, it's the Wilkes who found a medieval loophole to admit a
university-less German mathematician on the strength of one talk.
