# Finding the films — the PIXIE recovery saga, 2019–2020

The whole story of how the 1969 PIXIE demonstration films went from a rumor in a
handwritten talk script to published, scored, and beloved — told from the primary
correspondence (Don's full mail export, archived in the private repo). Companion to
[`../cambridge-films-flight-of-the-bumblebee.md`](../cambridge-films-flight-of-the-bumblebee.md),
which covers the *Flight of the PIXIE* production; this is the recovery timeline itself,
with dates and receipts.

## The timeline

**10 Feb 2019 — Don asks.** After eleven years of the film existing only as a line in
Buxton's 2008 notes ([the promise that went nowhere](buxton-2008-wiseman-notes/README.md)),
Don writes to the Cambridge University Library reference desk: *"Hello, honorable
researchers and librarians!"* — asking for the film, the full paper, and whether Buxton's
digitization offer ever produced anything findable. It hadn't: *"I can't find anything
about it on Buxton's web site billbuxton.com, or anywhere else on the net."*

**22 Feb 2019 — First answer: not in the archive.** David Chapman reports the film is
**not** in the Wiseman papers. But he's read Folder B.218 closely and extracts new facts
from the handwritten speech draft: the film was made for the Southampton CAD conference
"last year"; it was **the first film the team from the 4th floor had ever made**; the
first half was filmed in "the old building" (the original Mathematical Laboratory,
demolished when the Arup Building went up in 1969); and it showed a by-then outdated
version of PIXIE. The library doesn't hold the 1969 CAD Proceedings either. He closes:
*"If there is anything else you would like me to look for in the archives I would be
happy to try."*

**8 Mar 2019 — The breakthrough.** Chapman has worked two channels. The St John's College
Biographical Librarian is quietly tracing the rumor of William Newman's death (it came via
Bill Buckley; Newman was alive). And the Computer Laboratory's own Librarian has spoken to
a member of the **Rainbow group** — Wiseman's old computer graphics group —

> *"who has a copy of both of the Pixie films in his office."*

**Both** films. In an office. For fifty years. The Rainbow member offers — *unprompted* —
to have them digitized for the streaming media service, and Chapman connects him to the
University Libraries **Digital Content Unit**. Chapman: *"My job can certainly be very
interesting, particularly when, at times like this, I get the opportunity to look into a
subject which I previously knew very little about."*

**7 May 2019 — Firm quote.** Two pieces of news: William Newman is alive but seriously
unwell, in a care home (he died later that year). And the digitization has a firm quote
and permission — the films go off that week.

**9 Jul 2019 — Published.** Chapman: *"At long last the PIXIE demonstration films have
been digitised and made available online... I am sorry for the delay, but I hope they
were worth the wait."* And the ripple effect: *"The Librarian at the computer lab informs
me that **as a result of your enquiry they are considering digitising some of the other
films they have in their archive**."* One question at a reference desk started a
digitization program.

**18 Feb 2020 — The spark to publish.** A Max/MSP thread on HN surfaces Alan Kay's 1968
GRaIL demo film; Don posts the PIXIE films in reply
([22352536](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=22352536) — "Librarians rock") and mails
Alan Kay and HN's dan: the films are silent, and deserve context — maybe a highlights cut
with narration.

**21 Feb 2020 — "Hello, Doctor Lemke!"** Don's first email to Heinz, via CARS: would the
person who actually wrote the PDP-7 code explain what's on the screen — comments, or even
a voice-over? *"We have it so easy these days... standing on the shoulders of giants like
you and your colleagues at the Cambridge Computing Laboratory."*

**Feb–Mar 2020 — Heinz answers everything.** He identifies himself in the footage:

> *"Yes, I am the person in the two films working with PIXIE on the screen with the light
> pen — but looking much better in those times than today."*

He identifies the man walking through the door to the TTY as **John Hiles**, the third
author, who wrote the circuit-analysis application PIXIE drove — *"very regrettable he
died in 1972."* He reveals **PIXIE II**: after lying dormant for ~45 years, he revived the
concept in his own field of computer-assisted medicine — Lemke, Cypko, Warner & Berliner
(2014), *3D++ Visualisation of MEBN Graphs and Screen Representations of Patient Models
(PIXIE II)*, Stud Health Technol Inform 196:248–51. And he starts writing **"PIXIE History
— Back to the Roots (Part 1, 1967)"**, promising installments for 1968, 1969, 1970.

**28 Feb 2020 — "I have found the perfect music for the film!"** Don, to Chapman, Heinz,
and HN, with one link: Yuja Wang playing *Flight of the Bumble-Bee*. (A first cut used an
Orkestra Synthetique version — Chapman liked that one too, as capturing "the energy and
feeling I associate with the early days of computing.") Heinz weighs in: *"I am not so
sure whether I would not prefer the Yuja Wang version, an incredible performance"* — and
later, definitively: *"nothing beats Yuja Wang. What a choice you made integrating her
with PIXIE — or rather PIXIE with her."*

**17 Mar 2020 — Final cut.** Don incorporates Heinz's feedback and syncs the performance
to the footage in After Effects: [*Flight of the PIXIE*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jDrqR9XssJI),
dedicated to Wiseman, Lemke, Hiles — and Chapman.

**24 Mar 2020 — "Wow!"** Chapman, back from holiday into the UK's first COVID lockdown,
the library physically closed: *"The music and the cinematic effects you've used really
complement the original film... Like Professor Lemke, I am very much impressed by your
facility with Adobe Première!"* He's caught up on the whole Don–Heinz correspondence in
his absence, calls Part 1 of *Back to the Roots* fascinating, and tips Don to Cambridge's
own **Centre for Computing History** — "quite an impressive collection, including some
PDPs, although not the PDP-7 you are interested in sadly."

## Why this story matters

- **Eleven years vs. five months.** Buxton with Microsoft's money behind him: 2008–2019,
  nothing surfaces. Don plus one reference librarian: February to July 2019, both films
  found, digitized, and published openly — plus a follow-on program to digitize the rest
  of the lab's film archive.
- **The films were never lost.** They were in a Rainbow group member's office the whole
  time. Nobody had *asked the right person* — and the person who found the right person
  was a librarian following his own curiosity through the lab's social graph.
- **Recovery became relationship.** The ticket thread turned into a running exchange —
  Nero Wolfe fandom, Ted Selker's Joy Button, Raymond Scott, Douglas Adams's *Hyperland* —
  and Chapman became a named dedicatee of the film he helped rescue. The same thread
  delivered Heinz: correspondent, historian of his own work, and now the center of this
  room.
- **The enquiry is the intervention.** Both the digitization program and Heinz's
  *Back to the Roots* series exist because someone asked. Archives answer questions;
  they rarely volunteer.
