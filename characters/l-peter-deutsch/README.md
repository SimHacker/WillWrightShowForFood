## Who

**L Peter Deutsch** wrote **Basic PDP-1 LISP** at 17, finishing it in 1963: a Lisp 1.5 on a
4K-word PDP-1, and one of the first interactive read-eval-print loops. He went on to
Berkeley's SDS 940 timesharing system with Butler Lampson and Chuck Thacker, then to Xerox PARC,
where he worked on Interlisp and on Smalltalk's bytecodes and built **PS**, the Smalltalk-80
that translated bytecodes to native code as it ran, the ancestor of today's JIT VMs. Then Sun,
the Eight Fallacies of Distributed Computing, and **Ghostscript**, which put PostScript and PDF
everywhere for free. He now writes music.

## Why the cabinet

The cabinet is a live-coding emulator room for old machines, with source maps that run from a
word in core up through every form of its source to the scan of the page it was printed on.
It plans a **PDP-1**, and its first full showcase is **Basic PDP-1 LISP**, from the March 1964
document Peter wrote with Edmund C. Berkeley, *[The LISP Implementation for the PDP-1
Computer](http://s3data.computerhistory.org/pdp-1/DEC.pdp_1.1964.102650371.pdf)*. Run his REPL,
stop it on any instruction, and see that word's line of the listing, the rectangle on the scan,
the paragraph of the document that explains it, and what others have written about it since.
([cabinet TODO](../../packages/cabinet/TODO.md), [the model](../../packages/cabinet/DESIGN.md#source-maps-as-transclusion-layers-spans-links))

## PostScript, and Sun

Don wrote PostScript for a living, in NeWS, Sun's PostScript window system, and in HyperLook,
and Ghostscript is the free PostScript interpreter of those same years. Both were at Sun in
the NeWS era. There's a long conversation in that alone.
