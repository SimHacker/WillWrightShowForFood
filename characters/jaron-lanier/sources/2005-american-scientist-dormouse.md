## 1. Does UI history matter?

Lanier's opening: Shakespeare needs historical context; Tesla is optional for plugging in an AC outlet. Then he narrows:

> Does the history of computers as we experience them—the history of the user-interface design, for instance—matter? I say yes. Like Shakespearean English, the computer is a tool that must be understood in depth to be deeply useful, and the richer the information about context, the richer the understanding.

This is the warrant for the Engelbart hardware room and for talking to Jaron about the keyset, not just the mouse.

He calls it "bizarre" that a cultural history of the personal computer took this long. Markoff covers **1960–1975**, south of San Francisco around Stanford — later Silicon Valley. Computers and "a new culture of drugs, sex and rock and roll" were made "sometimes in the same rooms, by some of the same people." Computing design was "a central component of the 1960s counterculture in Northern California."

For engineering students: not titillation. Computers and the Internet are still changing; we can now see the gap between the designers' aspirations and mass use. "More inspirational than not," with "drama and lingering uncertainties."

## 2. Witness, 1980

Lanier arrived in Palo Alto in **1980**, after Markoff's window, and "got to know most of the people Markoff depicts." Verdict: Markoff **underplays** how outrageous they were; what Lanier saw was "said to have been mild compared with what had come before."

## 3. SAIL funk

The boomerang-shaped Stanford AI Lab in the hills: "a futuristic science-fiction vision and a dangerous, dilapidated mess" that would fail today's liability climate. Wires from hot machines "looking rather like the hair on the heads of the engineers." Ragged walls, hippie décor, "fragrance of marijuana and candles."

> And yes, there were drugs and naked people in the rooms where some of the code that now drives your e-mail around the globe was first set down.

Same social circles as the musicians who became the Grateful Dead and the people who "invented drug 'tripping' and New Age spirituality."

## 4. Open vs proprietary

Markoff's main ideological conflict, per Lanier: open vs proprietary software.

> the first open-source project, which was also the first video game: "Spacewar."

Early proprietary push: "a kid named Bill Gates."

Brand already sits on Spacewar / *Rolling Stone* 1972: [`../../stewart-brand/`](../../stewart-brand/).

Open layers we still breathe "reflect the cultural context in which they were invented."

## 5. Communal living → email architecture

In 1980 it was "somehow uncool not to live semicommunally." Interpersonal conflict "tore apart one group household after another in the 1980s."

> The architecture of e-mail as we know it was made up during the communal period; had the protocols been defined just a little later, a more realistic or even fatalistic model of human nature might have held sway.

Specifically: harder to falsify sender identity → less spam and viruses.

Adjacent to Jaron's later **data dignity** argument ([`../ideas.md`](../ideas.md) §6) without claiming the 2005 review already says "siren servers."

## 6. Who the book is about

Organizing portraits: **John McCarthy**, **Stewart Brand**. "The most beautiful and nuanced portrait" is **Douglas Engelbart**.

Engelbart "more than anyone else invented the modern user interface, modern networking and modern information management." The 1968 machine: "rudimentary implementations of a mouse, windows, word processing, databases, network file sharing." Transformative — "akin to the Moon landing, even if it wasn't as widely publicized."

## 7. Virtuosity and the half-born computer

> Engelbart wanted to build user interfaces to support virtuosity in users—the sort of virtuosity one would expect from a fine musician. But instead, to his profound disappointment, a compromise took hold in which only the less challenging of his ideas have come into widespread use.

> He and many of the other pioneers—Alan Kay and Ted Nelson, for example—labor on to this day, building the computer as they feel it should be, even as the world at large has adapted on a massive scale to a computer that to those pioneers is only half-born.

The chord keyset is the virtuosity half that did not ship. The mouse is the easy half that did.

## 8. People vs machines

Two cultures that looked compatible and were not: Engelbart / SRI (human-centered) vs SAIL AI.

> Engelbart once told me a story that illustrates the conflict succinctly. He met Marvin Minsky—one of the founders of the field of AI—and Minsky told him how the AI lab would create intelligent machines. Engelbart replied, "You're going to do all that for the machines? What are you going to do for the people?" This conflict between machine- and human-centered design continues to this day.

Lanier is the living witness of that telling. Memorial discussant for Marvin: [`../../marvin-minsky/ideas.md`](../../marvin-minsky/ideas.md).

## 9. Close

The book as "a hedge against complacency" for engineering students:

> How can they read these stories without wanting to prove that they can be more vital, revolutionary and inventive than a charming gang of hippies?
