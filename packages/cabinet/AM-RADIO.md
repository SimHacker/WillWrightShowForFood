# AM radio: hearing the cabinet

Hackers at MIT and the museums set a transistor radio on the PDP-7 and listened to the
interference it picked up from the running machine.
This is a proposal, for [Lars Brinkhoff](../../characters/lars-brinkhoff/README.md), SDF Vintage
Systems, the Computer History Museum and anyone else with a PDP-7, a Type 340 or a SIMH build: how
to get that sound back in an emulator, what we need from the real machine to do it accurately, and
which programs are worth listening to. The emulator is the cabinet, a PDP-7 and Type 340 that run
in a web page ([README](README.md)).

What grew out of it Don calls VM Radio, or EM Radio, for emulator: inspired by AM radio, but
tuned straight into the virtual machine and the programs running on it. It doesn't pick up
interference; it reads what the emulator knows. That means the program counter, the display
list, named words in core, the light pen, the front panel switches, and a game's state. Each
program gets its own soundscape. The bus dance below (approach 8, and "Built, first cut") is
VM Radio's first receiver.

## What exists already

- **SIMH** has the PDP-7 with a Type 340 and light pen. Phil Budne wrote the 340 for the PDP-6
  and PDP-10; Lars added the PDP-7 interface in [simh#752](https://github.com/simh/simh/pull/752)
  in 2019. Its only audio is `sim_video.c`'s SDL bell: a fixed 400 ms beep at 660 Hz. Nothing in
  open-simh models RFI, a speaker or a radio.
- **Lars on RFI**, when Don asked in March 2020 whether SIMH could generate or fake it: "That's an
  interesting thought. But before that I'd like to get the regular music players to work." By
  April he had "a PDP-6 simulator making noises" (the its-hackers post he linked is 404 now). His
  VT100 [terminal-simulator](https://github.com/larsbrinkhoff/terminal-simulator) synthesizes its
  bell and keyclick in `sound.c`.
- **Music on purpose, at MIT.** The AI Lab's PDP-6 had its own speakers. Guy Steele, touring it at
  about twelve: "The PDP-6 also had two speakers and a stereo amplifier sitting on top of it. The
  hacker typed a couple of commands on a keyboard, and the PDP-6 burst into a Bach Brandenburg
  concerto (no. 6, as I recall)." Lars posted it in
  [PDP-10/its#1339](https://github.com/PDP-10/its/issues/1339#issuecomment-428878039). Those are
  the "regular music players" he wanted working first: a machine playing music deliberately,
  beside the accidental music a radio picked up.
- **The P7 phosphor**: Lars's GLSL [crt-simulation](https://github.com/larsbrinkhoff/crt-simulation),
  and masswerk's PDP-1 Spacewar!, which Lars called the better P7 at the time.
- **The cabinet**: PDP-7, Type 340, light pen, teletype, paper tape and a Titan link, in
  TypeScript in the browser, running PIXIE from Heinz Lemke's 1972 listing, DEC's 1964 light pen
  diagnostic and DUEL. Lars in 2020: "I would like PDP-6/7/10 etc simulators to make the step to
  the web for people to more easily interact with historical software." This is one of those
  steps, and it has no sound yet.

## What the radio heard

The PDP-7's memory cycle is 1.75 µs: 571.4 kHz, at the bottom of the AM band, with harmonics at
1143 and 1714 kHz. Every cycle the core drivers and bus lines switch, and how much switches
(which address and data bits change, what the display is doing) sets the amplitude of those
harmonics. A radio tuned near one hears that amplitude as audio.

So pitch comes from loop length. A loop of N memory cycles repeats every 1.75·N µs and sounds at
571,428 / N Hz: 200 cycles is 2.9 kHz, 1000 is 571 Hz. Short loops are ultrasonic; the AM
receiver's ~5 kHz audio bandwidth throws them away and keeps the slower patterns. The LCM notes
credit the Type 347 controller, so display traffic belongs in the model as well as the CPU.
Everything in this section is a hypothesis until it is checked against the recordings.

## Prerequisite: real timing

Pitch is only right if time is. Today [cabinet.ts](src/cabinet.ts) counts one cycle per
instruction, and the applet runs `1000 / 1.75` of them per millisecond. The real machine takes one
memory cycle for operate and most IOTs, two for memory-reference instructions, one more for each
indirect, and more for EAE. The 340's display-file fetches take memory cycles from the CPU. Until
`step()` reports memory cycles and the 340 counts its fetches, every loop plays too high.

## Approaches, from physics to pure invention

1. **The radio.** A probe on the backplane scores each memory cycle, for example the bits that
   toggled on the address and data lines plus a display term. That stream at 571 kHz goes through
   a caricature receiver: the tuning dial picks a harmonic and a mix of line weights, a broadcast
   station near the harmonic adds the heterodyne whistle, then an IF filter, envelope detector,
   AGC, static and a small speaker. Turning the dial changes the timbre the way the real one did.
2. **A full IQ receiver.** The same stream as complex baseband through mixer, filter and AM
   detector, like [GNU Radio's AM example](https://wiki.gnuradio.org/index.php/Simulation_example:_AM_transmitter_and_receiver),
   in an AudioWorklet or offline in node. Accurate enough to compare with a recording, and at
   baseband no heavier than 1: Matt Kline's receiver ([below](#a-receiver-to-borrow)) is this
   design, so 1 and 2 become one.
3. **Real RFI from your own laptop.** [System Bus Radio](https://github.com/fulldecent/system-bus-radio)
   makes a computer's memory bus transmit AM, including [from a browser tab](https://fulldecent.github.io/system-bus-radio/).
   Drive it with the emulated PDP-7's activity envelope and an actual radio next to the laptop
   plays munching tunes. Weak and hardware-dependent (1580 kHz on the author's MacBook Air): a
   show stunt, not the default.
4. **Event sonification.** This is not a physical model. Each 340 display word is a grain (a point clicks, a
   vector chirps with length as duration and direction as pitch, a character is a formant), IOTs
   tick, interrupts thump, a light pen hit pings. Legible, and correct at any speed.
5. **Loop pitch.** Find the hot loop from the PC trace (its back edge, or autocorrelation), play
   571,428 / N Hz with a timbre from its instruction mix. This is a simplified radio model,
   computed from cycle counts, so it is correct at any emulator speed.
6. **Bytebeat.** Treat a register as a DAC: sample the AC, a chosen word, or the 340's x and y at
   each IOT or loop pass. Munching Squares' counter is already a bytebeat. The precedents are
   old: Peter Samson's PDP-1 music, CSIRAC in 1951, and Steve Dompier's Altair playing "Fool on
   the Hill" into an AM radio at the Homebrew Computer Club in 1975. A virtual speaker bit is a
   new device programs can drive on purpose, and Mitch's Forth can live-code it.
7. **Learned.** With calibration recordings, fit approach 1's weights, or a small model, from the
   bus-feature stream to the recorded spectrum, the same method as matching the P7 phosphor.
8. **The bus dance.** Approach 4 played as music rather than as clicks. Each processor on the
   backplane is a voice with its own register, timbre and scale: the PDP-7 low and percussive,
   the 340 a bright pluck, the [Forth device](FORTH-DEVICE.md) a chime, an Apple ][ its own
   instrument. You hear the CPU start the display, the display run its list while the CPU computes,
   and Forth chime in when it pokes memory: who is doing what, and who is waiting on whom.
   - **Where the sounds come from: Korz.** A listener is a slot installed in a layer above the
     ones it hears, as a patch is ([KORZ-INSPECT.md](KORZ-INSPECT.md)): it emits a grain and calls
     `next`, so the machine does exactly what it did before. Which slots it covers picks the
     activity: every memory-reference instruction, only IOTs to device 06, only the 340's vector
     mode. Installing or removing one recompiles the blocks it touches, as any patch does, and
     with no listener there is nothing to remove.
   - **Histograms, not only events.** Per audio frame, the counts the compilers already keep
     (each block's calls and instructions retired) become a chord: the hottest blocks are the
     loudest notes, so a program settling into its inner loop is heard as a drone, and a phase
     change as a chord change.
   - **Tuned, not discordant.** Pitches are quantized to a scale and grains to a beat grid, so
     address, opcode or vector direction choose the note within the key and the timing stays
     musical. That gives up accuracy to be legible, the opposite trade from approach 1, and both
     use the same probe.
   - **Built, first cut** (November 2026). [dance.ts](src/dance.ts) scores, in node with tests;
     `dance-player.js` and `bus-dance.svelte.js` in the app play it with Web Audio. A DANCE
     button on the PDP-7 and on the Korz Apple ][ turns it on. Built without the listener slots:
     the sources are counts the machines already keep, read each sixteenth (125 ms).
     - PDP-7: a bass from each compiled block's growth in instructions retired (from the PC
       between runs when the CPU is not compiling), a pluck from the 340's strokes by display
       block, a bell from the Forth device's counters.
     - Apple ][: a pluck from the 6502's blocks, a tick keyed to the Disk II's head while the
       motor runs, so a ProDOS or Forth boot plays the head stepping across the tracks, and a
       bell whose pitch follows the speaker's click rate.
     - Drums: four on the floor, hats thickening with the load, and a rim click every n
       steps, where n is the ratio of the two hottest blocks' work: a loop run three times per
       pass of its caller clicks three against the bar's four.
     - JAZZ: the blues scale, the second eighth of each beat a triplet late, a ride's
       spang-a-lang, a feathered kick.
     - An engine under it all, from two smoothed gauges: load is the emulated speed (.01× is
       none, 1× half, 100× full), fill a level. The PDP-7's is a saucer, two detuned sines
       rising with load, the warble widening with the 340's stroke count; the Apple's a diesel
       throbbing faster with load, its filter opening with Applesoft's free memory (`FRE(0)`'s
       STREND to FRETOP over TXTTAB to HIMEM).
     - Every voice has its own gain (`fade(voice, level, seconds)`), the bus a cross-fader,
       an [ECG](../ecg/README.md) or any program, can drive.
     - Harmony: a chord a bar, I vi IV V straight and ii7 V7 Imaj7 vi7 in jazz, stated by a
       soft pad when it changes. Every voice's notes are the chord's tones, so the machine's
       activity picks which tone and the progression decides which are on offer.
     - The person at the console bends it rather than playing it, the way computers answer
       in films: in time, in key, a beat or two later.
       - A key shifts the rim click's phase by a step and queues a three-note "beep boop bop"
         on the next offbeats, its notes from the chord and the key's code.
       - A light pen stroke is tension and release. Pressing and dragging builds tension by
         how far the pen travels; past 0.4 the harmony leaves the progression for the dominant,
         past 0.8 takes its flat ninth, and the hats and the engine are pushed. Lifting the pen
         resolves to the tonic on the next beat with a kick, and the progression starts over.
         A light touch fades back without resolving.
       - On the Apple, the mouse on the screen stands in for the pen.
     - LYDIAN, the devil's interval: major with its fourth raised a tritone above the tonic,
       the mode of the Simpsons theme. The vamp rocks between I with the raised fourth added
       and II major, whose third is that fourth, then II7. In JAZZ, full pen tension now takes
       the tritone substitution, D flat 7 for G7, and lifting the pen slides it down a half
       step home.
     - BOHLEN-PIERCE: 13 equal steps of the 3:1 tritave, which [Kees van
       Prooijen](https://www.kees.cc/music/scale13/scale13.html) ("13 tones in the 3rd
       harmonic") found in the seventies about when Heinz Bohlen and John Pierce did; it is
       known by their names, the [Bohlen-Pierce scale](https://www.huygens-fokker.org/bpsite/scales.html).
       Kees's own named scale, the Yaphi (six tones in the golden ratio), would make another
       style.
       His major mode is built as just major is, from triads on the tonic, dominant and
       subdominant, with 3:5:7 in place of 4:5:6 ([his modes](https://www.huygens-fokker.org/bpsite/modes.html)):
       1, 35/27, 7/5, 5/3, 9/5, 7/3, 25/9, steps 0 3 4 6 7 10 12. The dance plays his I IV V I
       cadence (I is 1 : 5/3 : 7/3, his dominant sits on 5/3), in bars of 13 sixteenths grouped
       4+3+3+3, on odd-harmonic waves, and the pen's tension pulls to his dominant.
     - EN0 to EN3: four generative layers after Brian Eno, each a bit, on or off by itself, so
       sixteen mixes; four buttons in a row on both the PDP-7's page and the Apple's. Each is a
       different track at its own time scale, every note in the chord in force, the lengths
       primes so nothing lines up with the bar or the others. EN0, *Music for Airports*' tape
       loops: five of 23, 29, 31, 37 and 41 sixteenths, a tone of the chord each. EN1, a drone:
       53 and 59, an octave below the pad, after *Discreet Music*'s slow tape delay. EN2, a
       sparkle: chimes two octaves up at 43, 47 and 61. EN3, voices: three singers at 67, 71
       and 73, after *Music for Airports* 2/1. A screen angel's mode may still say
       `loops: true`, which keeps the layers already on, or EN0 if none are.
     - Watches: words in core heard as tracks, by name (the DANCE row's listen field). A name
       is a label or a Forth variable or constant found in the dictionary
       (`src/forth-dict.ts`, both kernels' header layouts). Each gets a quiet beat loop 3 to 7
       sixteenths long, its timbre and pan from the name. The loop measures the word against
       its own typical change, so a heading moving by degrees and a pointer moving by words
       count steps of the same size. Each step moves the loop's phase and its note one chord
       tone, up as the word grows and down as it shrinks. While the word changes, the loop plays twice as often; its size, by powers of
       64, sets the octave. Each program names its own:
       - Forth: `tx ty th dl> dp rfree rp sp`, the turtle, its display list growing, the
         dictionary and the ring space filling, and both stacks' depths. The PDP-7 has no stack
         in its instruction set (JMS stores the return address in the subroutine's first
         word), but Forth keeps one: `ip`, `rp` and `sp` name the auto-index cells 010-012
         it keeps its pointers in, so a nested call climbs and its return comes back down,
         and `2 3 +` goes up two pushes and down one.
         An octal number watches that address;
       - LANDER: `h2 v fuel secs`;
       - HILO: `guess!0-99 secret~0-99`, which gives the number away. That is the joke: hearing
         it is one more channel. Both words are sung (`SUNG` in dance.ts), not looped: each
         value is its own pitch, three octaves from 0 to 99, off the scale. Your last guess
         sings at once and then once a bar; the secret is whistled every fourth bar. A right
         guess is a unison.
     - The pen's own tracks, played at once rather than on the beat, so the picture is an
       instrument you play by stroking it, button up. Every stroke of the last frame within 24
       points of the pointer sounds a rising arpeggio of the chord as the pen moves along it,
       0 to 1, so stroking along a line climbs and the next stroke starts low again. Untagged
       strokes drawn later in the frame start higher, so a turtle's spiral is a harp. The pen
       has instruments of its own that nothing automatic plays, so it stands apart from the
       melody and the drums: hovering is a harp; holding the button a resonant sawtooth lead,
       its filter opening, while the press builds tension, until it is lifted. Setting the pen
       down rings an FM bell at its height, lifting it at its x.
     - The hand is a harp too. A stroke's display tag (tags.fs) names its finger, "Thumb" to
       "Little finger", and each finger is a string a step above the last. Forth's SPOTS holds
       where the landmarks were drawn; on a fingertip (landmarks 4, 8, 12, 16, 20) the pen lands
       a tom with a tick on top and a bell for that finger. The tracks are checked every frame, not only when
       the pointer moves, so moving your fingers under a still cursor strums them.
     - Screen angel modes: a state in ANGEL.yml can carry `music: { style, bpm, loops, listen,
       mute }`, set on entering it. Little Computer People boots to the 6502 and the disk with
       no chords, asks your name over a soft jazz ride, the date in Bohlen-Pierce, waits for
       him to move in on Eno's loops over the engine at max, and plays jazz once he is home.
       Next: watches on the Apple, so a mode can listen to the game's own memory.
     - Apple prompt songs, by the character in PROMPT ($33): Applesoft's `]` is sunny,
       straight time at 96 with the Eno loops, and jazz at 132 while a program runs (CURLIN's
       high byte, $76, not $FF). Integer BASIC's `>` is techno, straight at 132 without them.
       The Monitor's `*` (CALL -151) is dark and industrial: Bohlen-Pierce on odd-harmonic
       waves at 72, as deep as the harmony goes. A BASIC program's length (TXTTAB to VARTAB,
       or PP to HIMEM) is a watched track and the depth: as it gets longer, the pad doubles
       an octave down, then two.
     - The PC's heat map, like a source map for sound: memory in named regions (the Apple's
       program, DOS, slot ROMs, Applesoft and Monitor; the PDP-7's 1K pages), each warmed by
       its share of the CPU's hits and cooling over a few seconds. Each warm region hums a
       chord tone of its own on the bar, as loud as it is warm, so where the machine spends
       its time crossfades slowly instead of flickering with every block.
     - Finishes: a program can say when a game or round is over (`finish` in
       cabinet-programs.js), with a score 0 to 1, and the dance plays a hooray: a brass
       arpeggio climbing the home chord over a kick, more notes, higher and louder the better
       the score, a snare roll past 0.8, panned toward whoever won. HILO's play-again wait is
       read from core (`hiloFinished`: getc's return address in waitcr, waitcr's at the JMP
       GAME after RIGHT), and the fewer the guesses, the bigger the hooray. DUEL's halt, as
       the halt explainer reads it, is a win to one side or a smaller one for a collision.
     - The harmony walks (`Harmony` and `Dance.phrase` in dance.ts). Each style is a graph of
       chords and the moves between them: major with the flat seventh and the minor iv
       borrowed, jazz with secondary dominants, the backdoor bVII7 and I7 into IV, minor with
       the half-diminished ii and harmonic minor's V, Lydian's I, II, iii, V, vi and vii, and
       Bohlen-Pierce's three. The first four bars are the style's own loop; every phrase after
       is a four-bar walk through the graph, picked from every walk that fits by a hash of the
       code the machine has run (the lead voice's keys, folded in beat by beat), the phrase's
       number and the switch word. Even phrases start at home, odd ones end on a chord that
       leads home, and a chord holds a second bar only when no walk fits. A resolve starts the
       loop again. So the same machine doing the same thing plays the same changes, and code
       that runs differently takes the harmony somewhere else.
     - On the Apple, Return in the screen angel's empty chat field flips one of the panel's
       120 bits at random, and so twists the dance.
     - The Apple's panel, `$C041`–`$C04F` (korz/apple2/README.md), twists the music the
       same way: its 120 bits are folded into one 18-bit word (`switchWord`). The program can
       set them too, so a program can turn the twist itself.
     - The front panel's switches twist the music (`twist` in dance.ts). Not a knob a switch:
       the 18-bit word is hashed, so any combination is its own and flipping one gives
       another. The hash picks a skew of the off sixteenths and a phase turning the drums and
       the gate round, then effects: a gate thinning the machine's voices to a pattern that
       keeps every beat, an echo from the other side, a ratchet at the half, every other beat
       up an octave (a tritave in Bohlen-Pierce), the instruments handed round, left and right
       swapped. All off is the plain dance, and the console shows what the switches are doing
       next to the dance controls. Programs that read the switches as controls, DUEL's ships,
       Spacewar's, change the music as they are played.
     - Routines called (`APPLE2_CALLS` in dance.ts): the Apple's documented Monitor and
       Applesoft entry points, each with a sound, checked against the ][+ ROM's bytes. Hits,
       once in a beat the routine was entered: BELL1 ($FBDD) beeps at about 1 kHz like the
       Apple, HOME ($FC58) crashes a cymbal, SCROLL ($FC70) kicks and turns the drums a step
       (the screen's line feed), Applesoft's ERROR ($D412) is a sad trombone, GARBAG ($E484),
       the garbage collector, rumbles. Rates, by calls a beat: NEWSTT ($D7D2), a statement run,
       drives the drums; COUT ($FDED) types on the keys; HPLOT ($F457) and PLOT ($F800)
       sparkle. Compiled, a call is a run of the block starting at the routine, since a JSR's
       target starts a block, so it costs nothing; stepped, it is the PC landing on the entry.
       The slow trace speeds run one instruction at a time even with the compiler, and count
       PCs, so the calls go on sounding as the code walks by line by line. After a gap the
       counts are history and the first reading is dropped.
     - Each sound has the speeds it plays at. The Apple's own speaker plays from 0.01× to 4×
       (`SPEAKER_SPEEDS` in korz-apple.svelte.js): slower, its flips would be a click an
       instruction, not a tone, so it goes quiet and the dance and the calls carry on. Its
       samples go through a cone that comes back to rest, so a speaker the program leaves alone
       is silence, and a gap between buffers makes no edge to click on.
     - The dance remembers (bus-dance.svelte.js, in the browser's storage): whether it is on,
       for every machine, so going from the PDP-7 to the Apple keeps dancing; and the style,
       tempo and loops chosen, for each machine. It starts off, so nobody is blown away by a
       first visit, and a page that comes back on starts its sound at the first click or key,
       as browsers require. A program's words to listen to stay the program's.
     - Riffs (`Riff` in dance.ts): a mode can bring a bar that plays itself, bass, drums and a
       hook in the progression's chord, heard even while the machine only waits for a key. The
       Apple's prompts each have one, on an instrument no other track uses: Applesoft's `]` is
       sunny, a bouncing bass under an organ skank on the off-beats, in C major; a running
       Applesoft program walks a jazz bass; Integer BASIC's `>` is techno, four on the floor
       under a sixteenth acid line, in C minor round the Aeolian loop; the Monitor's `*` is
       industrial, a broken kick and anvils in bars of 13. The two BASICs share a tempo, 112, so
       they differ by sound, not speed, and while a riff plays it is the only drums. Away from
       the prompts the riff stops. The dance and the Apple's speaker go quiet
       while the page's tab is hidden, and come back with it.
     - The teletype is a sequencer (`printed` in dance.ts): what it prints, and keys' echo on
       the paper, plays a character a sixteenth. A line feed kicks and turns the drums'
       pattern a step, the UNIX shell's `@` is a snare, spaces are hats, and other characters
       play chord tones on a tine piano of its own. Text that floods in plays several to a step
       to catch up. A line of nothing but note names a to g, `-` holds and spaces (`'` and `,`
       jump an octave) is a tune: it plays as written over the home chord. The UNIX demo writes
       Daisy Bell with `ed`, the song the IBM 7094 sang at Bell Labs in 1961, and `cat` plays
       it; then UNIX assembles its own kernel and `nm` prints the symbols the dance watches:
       `uniqpid` climbing with every command, `u.pid`, `.insys` while in a system call.
     - DUEL as a duet: no listing survives, so its ships' words were found by holding each
       control and diffing core (tapes/duel/README.md, `DUEL_SYMBOLS`): position, heading,
       velocity and torpedoes for each ship. A watched name beginning `left` or `right` pans
       that way.
     - The Forth demo has DELAY ( n -- ), a loop inside a loop whose number sets how often
       the inner one runs, and a slow squiral that waits `I 1 + DELAY` after each stroke:
       slow the machine down and hear the pauses grow with the spiral.
     - Tempo from 40 to 200 beats a minute, and the machine speed slider in 1-2-5 steps
       from .001× to MAX, so a slowed machine can be heard as phrases and a fast one as texture.
     - Not yet: the listener slots, a mixer on the page, more gauges (free core, the 340's
       duty cycle, interrupts per second), the time stretch below, and **front panels**: user
       definable panels of blinking lights and buttons that show the dance's state (the chord,
       the tension, the loops' phases, each voice's level) and that you press and play to change
       the music, the PDP-7's own console lights and switches among them.

## A receiver to borrow

Matt Kline's [Simulating Airband AM Radios](https://bitbashing.io/am-radio.html) (Bit Bashing,
19 June 2026, CC BY-SA 4.0) builds the receiver approaches 1 and 2 need, for
[OpenFreq](https://github.com/UOAF/OpenFreq), a radio voice chat for the Falcon BMS flight sim.
The code is [RadioPlayback.cs](https://github.com/UOAF/OpenFreqAudio/blob/develop/OpenFreqAudio/RadioPlayback.cs)
in OpenFreqAudio (C#, MPL 2.0), with a [demo app](https://github.com/UOAF/OpenFreqAudio/releases/tag/nightly).
What carries over:

- **Baseband, not RF.** Nothing runs at MHz. Each carrier is a phasor at its offset from the
  dial, scaled by its amplitude; sum the I and Q of all of them, add independent I and Q noise,
  and the envelope is sqrt(I² + Q²). Sum before taking the length, since the length is nonlinear.
- **Integrate phase**, θ += 2π·Δf / Fs each sample, instead of computing 2π·Δf·n / Fs, so turning
  the dial never jumps phase.
- **AGC** is a one-pole filter on the envelope, α = 1 − e^(−1 / (Fs·τ)), with one τ when the
  envelope rises and another when it falls; the output is envelope divided by AGC. His τ: 1 ms
  attack, 33 ms decay, a third of the 3 ms and 100 ms he found in radio specs. AM broadcast sets
  are quoted at 0.1–0.3 s decay, so the pocket radio probably wants a slower one.
- **Squelch** on mean power, with two detectors: τ 10 ms opens the gate, so impulse crackle can't
  break it; τ 2 ms closes it, so a strong signal doesn't leave a long tail; it closes 3 dB below
  where it opens, so it doesn't chatter; it opens at +6 dB over noise. The click when a signal
  arrives and the "kssh" when it goes are not effects: they fall out of AGC and squelch.
- **Band-pass** after the gate: a 3rd-order Butterworth high-pass at 300 Hz, then a 6th-order
  low-pass at 3 kHz (an FIR would need over 512 taps). Then a peak limiter, because clipping pops
  are broadband and break the illusion.
- **Noise** is Gaussian hiss plus Poisson crackle, not plain white noise. **Modulation index** 0.95.

What it changes here:

- **An envelope detector ignores tuning offset.** One carrier 100 Hz off the dial demodulates the
  same as one dead on. So the heterodyne whistle needs a second carrier, beating at the
  difference, and the obvious one is the broadcast station on that frequency, which any radio in
  the room was also hearing. Tuning then behaves as it did: the station, a whistle sliding in
  pitch as the dial nears a harmonic, the louder signal modulating the weaker, the machine's tune
  ring-modulated by the beat. Detuning alone only changes how much of the sidebands the passband
  keeps, which still colors the timbre, more gently.
- **The sources map straight across.** Each memory-cycle harmonic h·571,428 Hz near the dial is
  one of Kline's transmitters, and the probe's activity score, decimated to 48 kHz, is its
  amplitude: the mean is the carrier, the wobble is the modulation, and it never goes negative.
  The harmonic's weight is its SNR. Harmonics are 571 kHz apart, so only one is ever in the
  passband; a second clock, if the 340 has one, would be a second transmitter.
- **Squelch is per radio.** The Yaesu FT1XD in the Life video has squelch; the small AM set in
  the Munching video almost certainly has only AGC. If the timing chain stops at HALT, the carrier
  drops and the static rushes in, and on the Yaesu that ends in the kssh and silence.

## Speed, gaps and granular synthesis

The applet runs the machine in bursts per animation frame, at speeds from 0.001× to unthrottled.
At 1× a frame produces about 16.7 ms of emission, posted to an AudioWorklet with a ~50 ms jitter
buffer (postMessage; WEB-BENCH rules out SharedArrayBuffer). At other speeds there are two
choices:

- **Tape speed.** Pitch follows speed, like a tape played fast or slow. At 10× munching tunes go
  ultrasonic; at 0.1× they drop to a rumble.
- **Time stretch.** Granular synthesis: cut the 1× audio into 20–50 ms Hann-windowed grains and
  overlap-add them at the playback rate, aligning grains by autocorrelation (WSOLA) so tones do
  not warble. Slow speeds repeat grains, so a machine you are single-stepping still plays the
  sound of the loop it is in. Fast speeds skip them. A stall or dropped frame is filled with the current texture
  instead of a click or silence.

When the machine is paused, the last grains decay slowly, the audio counterpart of the P7
afterglow the tube already shows.

## Shape in the code

- **Probe** (backplane): the CPU reports each memory cycle (address, word, kind); the 340 reports
  its fetches and deflection events. Costs nothing when no probe is attached.
- **`src/radio.ts`**: pure functions from emission to samples, run in node. Decimation is a box
  filter of about 11.9 cycles per 48 kHz sample; after that, Kline's chain: phasors, envelope,
  AGC, squelch, band-pass, limiter. Tests render WAV snapshots beside the SVG ones and check with
  Goertzel that a loop of N cycles peaks at 571,428 / N Hz.
- **Worklet** (applet): jitter buffer, tuning, AGC, noise, granular stretch.
- **UI**: a radio with a tuning dial, volume, tape or stretch, and antenna placement near the CPU
  or near the 347.

- **Spirograph**, the LCM's other demo: source unknown; ask, or recreate.
- **Life**, as on sn 129: 36 × 36, with a Gosper glider gun. It is also a
  [Korz case study](https://github.com/SimHacker/moollm/blob/main/designs/korz/case-cellular-automata.md).
- **Minskytron and the Minsky circle** (HAKMEM 149): two instructions per point, clean loops, so
  clean tones.
- **DUEL** (1968), already on the cabinet: hear the torpedoes.
- **HILO and LANDER** on the teletype: slow, bursty loops; a good test of the granular stretch.
- **Music on purpose**: a virtual speaker bit, and bytebeat live-coded in Mitch's Forth.
- **Sound studies, written to explore the radio.** The AM radio picks up the
  rhythm of memory cycles, so a loop of n cycles is a pitch, how long it runs is a duration, and
  what it touches is a timbre. A set of small programs maps the space:
  - **Pitch**: loops of exact cycle counts, a scale and a sweep, one note per second.
  - **Timbre**: the same pitch with the 340 drawing a point, a vector, a character, or nothing;
    with and without EAE multiplies, memory-reference against operate instructions.
  - **Envelope and rhythm**: notes started and stopped on clock ticks; rests by halting the
    display; accents from brighter, longer 340 bursts.
  - **Texture**: a DLA or a CA running, which makes noise with structure.
  
  Each runs in the emulator through `radio.ts` and, if the museum records it, on sn 129, so every
  study is also a calibration point for approach 7.
- **Percussion and voice, mixed over the radio.** The machine plays the bass and the noise; a
  drum track and a sung or spoken part, cleared for YouTube, go over it in the edit, cut to the
  same cycle timeline the demo runs on. A Forth word can mark the beats (`beat` tick on the
  clock), so the human parts lock to the machine and not the other way round.

## Discussion: which approach, and when

The approaches answer different questions and can share one probe.

- **Faithful or legible.** The radio (1, 2) is the only one that could sound like the recordings.
  Sonification (4) and loop pitch
  (5) are easier to hear things in: you can tell a vector from a point, or the inner loop from the
  outer one, which RFI smears together. A museum exhibit wants the first; a debugger wants the
  second. Build the probe once and offer both.
- **Physics we don't have.** How strongly each bus line and the 347's deflection drivers radiate
  is unknown. Approach 1 turns that into a few weights and a dial; 7 fits them to recordings. The
  order is to build 1 with guessed weights, compare it with the recordings, then ask for
  measurements.
- **Time.** Anything computed from memory cycles (1, 2, 5) is only right once the timing is right,
  and is independent of how fast the host runs. Anything computed from wall-clock bursts is wrong
  at every speed but 1×. Granular stretch keeps the cycle-exact audio usable at other speeds.
- **The same design fits SIMH.** SIMH's CPU loop can call the probe at each memory cycle, and its
  SDL layer already opens an audio device for the bell. `radio.c` would be `radio.ts` in C, fed
  from `PDP18B/pdp18b_cpu.c` and `display/type340.c`. We would rather share one model than grow two.

## What we would ask of museums with a running machine

The model's weights can only be set from recordings of a real machine. For SDF's sn 129, or any
PDP-7, PDP-9 or PDP-10 with a 340:

1. **A known program.** Munching Squares with its switch settings written down, or better, a
   test tape we supply: loops of 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 memory cycles, each held for ten
   seconds, then the same loops with the 340 drawing a point, a vector and a character.
2. **A known radio.** Make and model, AM, the frequency in kHz, and a photo of where it sat.
   Sweep the dial slowly once while the tape runs, and say the frequency aloud as you go,
   including where it crosses a broadcast station.
3. **Good audio.** The radio's headphone or line output into a recorder at 48 kHz, not a phone
   microphone across the room. A second track from a mic is welcome too.
4. **Optional.** An SDR (an RTL-SDR with an upconverter covers 500 kHz to
   1.8 MHz) recording IQ near the machine. That would show the harmonics directly and settle the
   weights in approach 1.

We would publish the recordings with credit, and the cabinet would ship the same test tape so
anyone can compare the emulator against the machine.

## Order of work

1. Memory-cycle timing in the PDP-7 and 340.
2. Probe and `radio.ts`, with node WAV tests.
3. Munching Squares on the PDP-7.
4. Worklet and the radio UI.
5. Granular time stretch.
6. The calibration test tape; compare with the three recordings, then ask SDF to record it.
7. Stunts: System Bus Radio, a real radio at the show.
8. VM Radio at a crawl: instruments that change with the speed. At speed the dance polls once a
   beat: the Korz compiler's block counts, named words, the devices. Thousands of accesses blur
   into texture there, and that is right. Very slow, below about 0.05x and while stepping or
   tracing, single events can be heard, and a hook on every access costs nothing that matters:
   at 0.01x the PDP-7 runs about 5,700 instructions a second. There, observe-layer watch slots go
   into the Korz sea (KORZ-DISPATCH.md): a fetch at a labelled routine (UNIX's `putchar`, HILO's
   `right`) is a hit, a write to a watched word sings its new value on that cycle, and `who`
   gives DMA and the 340's display-list reads voices of their own. The two cross-fade with the
   speed, so slowing down lets single events step out of the texture and speeding up melts them
   back in, as the Apple's speaker now clicks at any slow speed. Only the Korz rungs have slots;
   the plain interpreters and microM8 keep the polling.
